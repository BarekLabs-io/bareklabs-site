§0 · Key findings

The economy is inevitable; the entry points are not

Five findings — and three corrections to what you may have read this week.

  • 01 — The cost of orbit fell ~95% in a generation and is about to fall again. Shuttle-era launch ran ~$54,500/kg; a reused Falcon 9 delivers at ~$2,400/kg; Starship's design target is under $100/kg. Flight 13 (24 July 2026) deployed 20 production Starlink V3s and returned the ship intact — the next cost leg is now flying real hardware.
  • 02 — Starlink proved a space business can be a great business. FY2025: $11.4B revenue, $4.42B operating income, ~39% margin, 10.3M subscribers — the proof of concept the entire sector trades on. Watch ARPU: $99 → $66 as growth shifts down-market.
  • 03 — Defense is the quiet demand floor. Golden Dome carries a CBO estimate of $1.2T over 20 years, ~70% of it a ~7,800-satellite interceptor constellation. Rocket Lab is on the SBI program; ASTS wins awards through primes.
  • 04 — The IPO repriced everything. SPCX is 15.2% below its $135 IPO price at ~$1.44T; July took SPCX −35%, RKLB −39%, ASTS −29%; ~930M locked-up shares (~$100B) become eligible 6 August 2026.
  • 05 — No name is justifiable on 10-year cash flows. Illustrative DCFs: RKLB ≈ $8.5B vs $39.5B EV; ASTS ≈ $8.8B vs $19.5B EV. These are options on the 2035 economy, priced in 2026 dollars — narrative durability is the floor.

Why this note, why now. Space is having its loudest year in public markets — a trillion-dollar IPO, rockets on the evening news, a new space ticker in every feed. But the louder the buzz, the thinner the public knowledge behind it: most of what circulates is render videos, slogan valuations and rumor-grade headlines. So we decided to write about it — properly. What orbit actually costs, what these businesses actually earn, and what the stocks actually price in. Every number on this page drills down to a dated source; where we exercise judgment, we say so.

§2 · The physics

Reusability is the business model; everything else is a derivative

The Shuttle promised $600/kg and delivered $54,500. Falcon 9 actually delivered ~$2,400. Starship is the option on $100.

Launch was a government-procurement oligopoly — cost-plus contracts, no reuse, no cadence — and it priced orbit as a luxury good. SpaceX landed its first orbital booster in December 2015; 85%+ of its missions now fly on previously flown boosters, and it flew 165 times in 2025 — nearly eight times the next-busiest Western provider. The market implication is binary and underappreciated: Starship is not needed for SpaceX's existing economics — it is the option on the next ones (V3 constellation capacity, gen-2 direct-to-cell at 150 Mbps, orbital compute). Flight 13 says the physics is cooperating; two lost boosters say the economics are not yet bankable.

§3 · Three actors

SpaceX: the franchise is the best in the sector; the price is the worst

One profitable franchise, a strategic launch utility, and an AI furnace — consolidated.

Starlink is the ontology breaker: a space-native service with recurring revenue, consumer scale and 39% operating margins — $11.4B FY2025 revenue growing ~50%, 10.3M subscribers across 155 countries. It also generates all of SpaceX's profit, funding ~$3B of Starship R&D and absorbing a consolidated xAI whose largest contract — up to $45B from Anthropic through May 2029 — is 90-day cancellable. Build $1.44T from parts and you find ~$0.5–0.9T of defensible value; Morningstar's $62 fair value lands inside that range. The supply schedule is the H2 story: ~930M shares unlock 6 August, a performance tranche sits at $175.50, and Musk cannot sell for 366 days.

Rocket Lab: the best executor, priced for a flawless Neutron

Revenue +63% and $1.38B of cash — the market is paying for the rocket that hasn't flown.

Rocket Lab is the sector's cleanest execution story: record 21 Electron launches in 2025, Q1 2026 revenue of $200.3M (+63% YoY), cash of $1.38B after converts, a record $266M Space Force missile-defense launch contract, 18 iQPS missions booked, and a VICTUS HAZE responsive-launch demo that flew 16 hours 42 minutes after notice. At $70.43 the stock trades at ~58× TTM sales — a multiple that embeds Neutron flying on time (Q4 2026) and Golden Dome enduring. The Neutron debut is a coin-flip the stock cannot afford to lose. Q2 prints 10 August.

AST SpaceMobile: the cleanest expression of the core trade

Verified physics, binary schedule, 230× sales — and a week that can double or halve the story.

ASTS owns the sector's most visceral technology demo — broadband to an unmodified phone from orbit — and its most binary schedule risk. Ten BlueBirds operate today (BB7 was destroyed on New Glenn's NG-3 failure in April); BB11–13's launch window opened 5 August; the 45-satellite target slipped from year-end 2026 to early 2027 on 15 July, the same week the company priced $1B of 1.625% converts due 2034. Cash is $3.03B against $2.99B of debt; short interest is ~20% of float. At 230× TTM revenue, the equity requires the 2027–2028 ramp to arrive on time.

§4 · Landscape

A monopoly with a queue, and a four-way race for the phone in your pocket

Launch is SpaceX's to lose; direct-to-cell is the demand frontier where nobody has won yet.

SpaceX's 165 launches in 2025 were nearly eight times the next-busiest Western provider. Blue Origin's New Glenn has flown three times, failed on NG-3 (destroying ASTS's BB7), and lost a vehicle to a ground-test explosion in June; Europe's Ariane 6 sits near ~$7,500/kg; China's LandSpace targets a Zhuque-3 reuse attempt in H2 2026 — the first credible non-US answer to Falcon 9. §5 · Catalysts & risks

A dated ladder of binaries

Three prints, three launches, one unlock — the next six weeks alone can reprice the whole sector.

  • R1 — Lockup absorption (SPCX). ~930M shares (~$100B) become eligible 6 August — roughly tripling float against a short base already ~1/3 of float. If the bid isn't there, the sector's benchmark resets lower.
  • R2 — Neutron debut (RKLB). A Q4 2026 slip or failure compresses the multiple toward the FLY/LUNR band (~16–20×) — the bear case is −73%.
  • R3 — Schedule credibility (ASTS). The 45-satellite target already slipped a year; a BB11–13 failure or a second guidance slip breaks the ramp against T-Satellite's 150 Mbps gen-2 clock.
  • R4 — AI multiple compression (SPCX). xAI's largest contract is 90-day cancellable; an AI capex revolt hits the consolidated P&L that justifies ~$100–200B of the valuation.
  • R5 — Golden Dome affordability. CBO's $1.2T estimate vs Guetlein's $185B is a genuine binary; a descope removes the demand floor the defense multiples rest on.

§6 · Valuation

Options on the 2035 economy, priced in 2026 dollars

Scenario frames, not price targets — each with its kill-switch.

We built simplified two-stage DCFs for both pure-plays: RKLB comes out near $8.5B against an actual EV of $39.5B; ASTS near $8.8B against $19.5B. No name in this sector is justifiable on ten years of discounted cash flow — the multiples embed Starship-era cost curves, D2C at full constellation, Golden Dome at full procurement. That does not make the stocks wrong; it makes them narrative assets. July 2026 was the dress rehearsal for what happens when the narrative breaks.

Final word

SPCX — the franchise is the best in the sector and the price is the worst; wait for the lockup to clear before building a position. RKLB — the best-executing company relative to its plan, at a multiple that leaves no room for the plan to change. ASTS — the only name where this week's news can genuinely double the equity story or halve it; sized as an option, it is the most interesting risk/reward in the sector. The economy is inevitable. These entry points are not.
