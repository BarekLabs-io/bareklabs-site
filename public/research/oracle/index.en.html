A year ago, if you'd asked me what I thought of Oracle, I'd have talked about databases and Java licensing. Today it's become one of the most contested tickers in American tech — and not for obvious reasons. The stock has lost more than half its value since its September peak. Its credit rating was downgraded this summer. There are articles about cost overruns on construction sites in Wisconsin and New Mexico. And yet the analysts covering this name still carry a consensus price target implying more than 70% upside. Something doesn't add up at first glance — and that's exactly the kind of situation worth opening the hood on rather than trusting the headline.

I'm not a software engineer and I'll never pretend to be one here. What I've done for roughly a decade is read P&Ls and balance sheets and run business operations at an international scale — inside environments where cloud, ERP and data infrastructure aren't abstract topics, they're budget lines you negotiate, vendors you audit, production outages you live through in real time. That doesn't make me a GPU expert. But it gives me an operator's eye on this kind of story — I know what it looks like when a vendor's pitch doesn't match the reality of a deployment, and I know how to read a balance sheet without being told a story.

So here's the plan: price action, FP&A mechanics (RPO, capex, cash flow, debt), the technical side (GPU depreciation, network infrastructure), the customer-concentration risk running through Stargate/OpenAI, and the uncomfortable question — is Oracle's AI cloud business even profitable. I'm not going to tell you to buy or sell. I'm going to give you the numbers, with sources, so you can make up your own mind.

Chapter 1

The wild ride

Few mega caps have had a year this bipolar. On September 9, 2025, ORCL closes at $228.60. The next day, the stock jumps 42% in a single session — Oracle has just disclosed RPO up 359% year-over-year to $455B, driven by a massive cloud contract with OpenAI. For a few hours, on paper, Larry Ellison becomes the richest person alive. September 10, 2025 remains the stock's all-time closing high to date: $324.63.

ORCL price at key dates, Sep 2025 → Aug 2026. Built from dated reference points (announcements, earnings, credit events), not a continuous daily feed.

Exhibit 1 — Price Action

From that peak, capex reality set in step by step. On June 10, 2026, FY26 results beat consensus on revenue and RPO — but FY27 capex guidance ($90-95B) sends the stock down in after-hours trading. On July 9, S&P downgrades the credit rating to BBB-, one notch above junk. Then come the articles about construction overruns. The stock hits a 52-week low of $114.50 on July 28 — down 56% from the September peak.

Since late July, it's turning around: a $7B Pentagon contract, Google's Gemini getting integrated into OCI, and Mizuho publishing a note arguing the stock could more than double from these levels. As of August 4, the stock trades at $144.39 — still down 28% for calendar 2026, but up 26% from its July low.

What strikes me most about this one

41 to 49 analysts cover the stock, consensus rating Buy, average target around $252 — ranging from $110 (CLSA, cautious) to $400 (Guggenheim, Jefferies, Global Equities Research). At $144, that's an implied upside of +74%, one of the widest gaps in all of large-cap tech.

Chapter 2

FP&A — the engine room

This is where the thesis actually lives. Three curves worth knowing cold: the RPO that's exploding, the capex chasing it with a lag, and the cash flow absorbing the difference.

The base, before AI

Oracle has been selling databases since 1977, and that software base still generates solid revenue and a gross margin no AI pure-play comes close to. Total revenue went from $42B in FY22 to $67.4B in FY26 — steady growth, until FY27 guidance ($90B, officially confirmed) marks a real change of slope.

Cloud mix is accelerating — the capex trajectory tells you where the growth is coming from

The RPO — the real backlog monster

Remaining Performance Obligations is the value of contracts already signed but not yet recognized as revenue. Its six-quarter trajectory is properly dizzying: stable around $130-138B through May 2025, then a jump of +$330B in a single quarter (Q1 FY26, the OpenAI reveal), before continuing to climb to $638B.

Quarterly RPO, in $B, dashed line = FY26 annual revenue for scale. The last six points come directly from Oracle's SEC 8-K filings; none are estimated.

Exhibit 3 — The Backlog Monster

The detail that changes how you read the RPO

Oracle's own 10-Q spells out the actual conversion pace: of the $552.6B in RPO as of February 28, 2026, only about 12% is expected to convert to revenue over the next 12 months (~$66B), 31% over months 13-36, 35% over months 37-60, and the remainder beyond that. Applying the same logic to $638B, the "next 12 months" slice is probably around $75-85B. Total RPO is a 5-plus-year backlog, not a one-year revenue forecast.

The capex trap

$21.2B in FY25. $55.7B in FY26 — already $5.7B above Oracle's own guidance, itself raised several times from an initial $35B. And now $90-95B targeted for FY27, confirmed in the 10-K filed in late June.

Operating cash flow, capex and free cash flow, trailing twelve months (TTM), by quarter. FY26 Q4 = full fiscal year. Sources: Oracle quarterly releases, 10-Q/10-K.

Exhibit 4 — The Capex Trap

OCF is growing (+54% for FY26), but capex is growing faster, quarter after quarter, since early FY25. Oracle is investing every dollar it generates — and borrowing the rest. To close the gap: $43B in debt and $5B in equity raised in FY26, and roughly $40B more planned for FY27 (including a $20B at-the-market equity program), with no new bond debt expected in calendar 2026.

Methodology note: leases have sat on the balance sheet since ASC 842, so "off-balance-sheet" isn't quite right for the debt itself anymore. What does stay off-balance-sheet: the special purpose vehicles (SPVs) Oracle uses to finance some data centers through third parties — Blue Owl, Crusoe, JPMorgan among others, roughly $13B, $38B and $18B across Texas, Wisconsin and New Mexico sites.

D/E actually improved between May 2025 and May 2026 (452% → 344%) — equity nearly doubled (net income up 36%, plus a $5B preferred stock issuance in February). Still: S&P downgraded Oracle from BBB to BBB- on July 9, one notch above junk, citing adjusted debt/EBITDA reaching 4.3-4.5x in FY27 (vs. 3.3x in May 2025) and a free cash flow deficit that could double to -$42B. Moody's has it on negative outlook. The 5-year CDS spread climbed to 203bp, an 18-year high.

Chapter 3

The engineering side — GPUs, depreciation, network

Two separate questions hide under "is AI cloud profitable": how long does a GPU stay economically useful, and does Oracle actually have an infrastructure edge or just an entry ticket bought with billions.

In November 2025, Michael Burry publicly accused hyperscalers of inflating earnings by depreciating GPUs over too many years against real-world obsolescence closer to 2-3 years. His estimate: up to 26.9% overstatement of Oracle's earnings by 2028 — the highest in the sector alongside Meta. Oracle depreciates servers over 6 years, unchanged since the 2022-2024 industry-wide extension. Amazon shortened part of its fleet to 5 years in 2025; Meta extended to 5.5. H100 rental rates fell from a peak of $8-10/hour to around $2.85-3.50/hour.

The defense

A bounded accounting estimate change (ASC 250), audited — not an error or fraud. Different operators can legitimately land on different useful-life assumptions.

The doubt

On $10B of GPUs, 3-year vs. 6-year depreciation swings operating income by roughly $1.8B/year — from an assumption, not a sale.

Free cash flow, indifferent to whichever schedule is chosen, remains the more reliable metric for judging Oracle today — far more than GAAP EPS, which grew 34% in FY26.

On infrastructure, the edge is real, not just marketing. SemiAnalysis awarded OCI a ClusterMAX Gold rating in its first ranking, with RDMA networking judged competitive against Nvidia's own Spectrum-X. Zettascale10 clusters scale to 131,072 interconnected Blackwell GPUs, latency claimed as low as 2.5 microseconds. GPU utilization hit 97.5% in Q4 FY26, with 92% of capacity renewed at expiration.

But physics catches up with the spreadsheet: the Wisconsin campus (~$15B, ~1GW) is in a dispute with the local power regulator, which requires guarantees above $100M/year from operators rated below A- — Oracle, at BBB-, is directly affected, and sued the regulator in June. Bloomberg reported Oracle pushed some OpenAI data center delivery from 2027 to 2028; Oracle denied it days later. Both versions coexist in the press; neither is independently verifiable from outside.

Chapter 4

Stargate & OpenAI — the real concentration risk

This is the crux of the whole thesis. The OpenAI contract: $300B over 5 years, 4.5GW of capacity, starting in 2027 — a run-rate close to $60B/year.

Key dated milestones, Jul 2025 → 2027 contract start.

Exhibit 6 — Stargate Timeline

A note on sourcing: the $300B figure first comes from the Wall Street Journal (September 10, 2025, citing people close to the matter), independently confirmed by CNBC — neither Oracle nor OpenAI has published that exact amount in a joint statement or SEC filing. What's officially confirmed on both sides: the 4.5GW of capacity and the 2027 start date.

On concentration: Bank of America estimated OpenAI accounted for more than 50% of total RPO at the Q4 results — one analyst floated a high-end estimate above 90% as early as September 2025. This is a factor S&P explicitly cited in its downgrade, and it's objectively risk number one here.

The problem isn't that OpenAI disappears — it's that its revenue isn't yet keeping pace. Mid-2026: roughly $25B annualized revenue, growing fast, but with an operating loss projected around $14B for 2026 alone. Positive cash flow isn't targeted until 2030.

The mechanism behind the risk

OpenAI's stated strategy is to keep its own balance sheet light — turning capex into multi-year purchase commitments instead of owning data centers. That shifts construction and leverage risk onto partners like Oracle. If OpenAI can't meet its commitments, Oracle holds purpose-built leases that are hard to re-let on equivalent terms. Exactly the point S&P flagged.

To OpenAI's credit: a record $122B funding round ($852B valuation), a confidential IPO filing targeting more than $1T (June 2026), and a renegotiated Microsoft deal capping obligations at $38B through 2030 — down from ~$135B. And the contract isn't purely a future promise: Abilene is already live, 1.2GW delivered and billed.

Chapter 5

TikTok, the Pentagon, Meta, Google — the diversification hand

The "Oracle = OpenAI bet" narrative is incomplete. Over twelve months, Oracle signed or deepened at least four relationships that reduce, on paper, its dependence on a single customer.

  • TikTok — the TikTok USDS JV closed January 22, 2026: Oracle, Silver Lake and MGX each hold 15%. Oracle hosts all U.S. data on OCI and oversees the algorithm's audit. Direct financial contribution isn't broken out publicly — the value here is reputational.
  • The Pentagon — July 23, 2026, the DoD awarded Oracle a framework agreement worth up to $6.9-7B over 10 years.
  • Meta — a cloud contract worth roughly $20B reportedly close to finalized.
  • Google — July 2026, Gemini integrated directly into OCI AI Agent Studio, putting Oracle alongside Meta, OpenAI and xAI as a multi-model host.

What I think the market underweights: Oracle is becoming neutral infrastructure for several competing AI labs at once, which mechanically dilutes OpenAI's relative weight over time — even if, today, it's still marginal next to Stargate's scale.

Chapter 6

Is AI cloud even profitable?

Honest answer: not yet at the group level, but the trajectory is measurable.

Margin comparison by source, in %. The three figures measure different scopes.

Exhibit 7 — Margin Reality Check

The worrying signal

Internal documents reported in the press point to an average 16% margin across AI cloud deals — well below the group's 65.8% gross margin.

The reassuring signal

Management pointed to a 32% gross margin on AI capacity delivered in Q3 FY26 — above its own 30% target.

Futurum Group's read seems right to me: the main drag isn't capacity already delivered and billed, it's carrying cost of capacity under construction at the same time — timing more than structural demand. Still, today's AI margin sits well below the group's historical average. That's a fact, not an opinion.

Chapter 10

Three ways this plays out

Not a prediction — three internally-consistent paths, so you can track which one the next few quarters actually confirm.

Bull

RPO-to-cash conversion accelerates, delivered-capacity margin holds above 30% as scale kicks in, OpenAI's IPO derisks the counterparty question, diversification keeps broadening. Re-rating toward consensus ($252) or higher becomes plausible.

Neutral

Backlog keeps growing but conversion stays slow, margins improve gradually, debt keeps climbing but stays investment grade. Stock chops in a wide $130-190 range for several quarters while the market waits for more data.

Bear

A large AI-lab customer hits a funding air-pocket, capex keeps outpacing conversion, FCF deficit widens toward S&P's -$42B scenario, a second downgrade triggers higher funding costs. Stock re-tests or breaks the $114.50 low.

Here's where I land: Oracle today is two companies inside one ticker. A real, profitable software base still gaining ground even inside its competitors' clouds. And a real infrastructure bet, funded with real leverage, whose profitability still has to prove itself quarter after quarter. Both are true at once. That's exactly why this stock makes so much noise — and exactly the kind of story I want to keep following here.
