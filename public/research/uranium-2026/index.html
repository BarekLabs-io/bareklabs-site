§ 00 — Executive summary

Six things the tape is pricing wrong

This is a sector where the headline price is not the price producers receive, where the largest buyer of physical uranium has switched itself off, and where the entire artificial-intelligence demand narrative generates almost no incremental uranium consumption this decade. The six

  • The term price has crossed above spot and stayed there. That is the defining structure of 2026. Utilities are paying a premium for delivery certainty, not for pounds. Spot is a residual market; term is where the sector's revenue is actually set.
  • No producer receives the spot price. Kazatomprom realised $67.88/lb in H1 2026 against a month-end spot average of $86.83. Cameco realised US$66.96/lb. Legacy contract books cap the upside for years. Every "uranium is at $86 so the miners are printing money" take is wrong by roughly a quarter.
  • Costs are inflating faster than the price. Kazatomprom's own 2026 C1 guidance is US$23.50–25.00/lb against an FY2025 actual of US$18.06 — up 30–38% at the lowest-cost operation on earth. Denison's Phoenix capital cost rose 20% in real terms against its own 2023 study.
  • The AI-nuclear deal wave creates almost no uranium demand before 2030. A power purchase agreement on an already-operating reactor is a margin and duration event for the utility and zero incremental fuel demand. Only three US restarts (~2,250 MW) sit inside the five-year window — and not one has delivered a single megawatt-hour.
  • The real bottleneck is downstream. Rosatom held 44.1% of world enrichment capacity on the last published count. US law terminates every Russian import waiver on 1 January 2028. Essentially no new Western separative work arrives before 2028, and the bulk lands 2030–2036.
  • The equities have de-rated harder than the commodity. Through 30 June, spot was +4.28% year-to-date while uranium mining equities were −3.91% and juniors −7.43%. Centrus is 58% below its 52-week high with 26% of its float short. This is not a melt-up. It is a de-rating inside a firming physical market.
Non-obvious angle n°1

The enrichment shortage is itself a uranium demand accelerant

Enrichment and uranium are substitutes at the margin. An enricher can run "underfed" — using more separative work and less natural uranium — or "overfed", burning more uranium to save SWU. The choice is pure arithmetic on relative prices.

Separative work last printed at roughly US$200/SWU (Urenco, end-2025) against uranium at US$86/lb. At that ratio the economics point hard toward overfeeding. That does two things simultaneously: it removes the secondary supply that underfeeding used to release into the market, and it raises the natural uranium required per fuel assembly. The enrichment squeeze is not a separate story from the uranium story. It is a uranium demand multiplier, and no public model we could find quantifies it. The last published estimate of the effect dates from 2015.

What this report will not do

It will not print a 2028 EBITDA estimate for a company that has not guided one, will not compute a cash cost for a producer that does not disclose one, and will not quote a price target as though it were a forecast. Where a number does not exist publicly, this report says so. There are more "not disclosed" statements in the pages below than in any sell-side note on this sector — that is the point. Roughly a third of the figures circulating in retail uranium research cannot be traced to a filing.

§ 01 — Price structure

Two uranium prices, moving in opposite directions

Uranium does not trade on an open market. Cameco says so on its own price page, verbatim. There is no exchange-cleared benchmark with meaningful depth; there are two subscription price reporters, UxC and TradeTech, and a handful of derived indicators. Everything downstream of that — every valuation, every screen, every headline — inherits that opacity.

The most useful free series is Cameco's, which publishes the month-end average of the two reporters for both spot and long-term. Over 2026 those two lines have gone in opposite directions, and that divergence is the single most informative thing in the sector.

Non-obvious angle n°2

No producer receives the headline price. Not one.

This is the most consequential misreading in retail uranium research, and it is trivially checkable in any quarterly report. Kazatomprom's group average realised price in H1 2026 was US$67.88/lb against a month-end spot average of US$86.83 — a 22% discount. Cameco's H1 realised price was US$66.96/lb. Ur-Energy realised $70.98. enCore realised $67.78 against a cost of sales of $68.02 — a negative gross margin on delivered pounds, in a $86 market.

The gap is contract structure. Books signed in 2019–2022 at $30–50/lb with escalators are still delivering. Base-escalated contracts fix the price at signature; market-related contracts float but carry ceilings. Cameco does not disclose the split between the two — a material omission, given that the split determines the entire earnings sensitivity of the company. The correct mental model is that a producer's realised price is a slow-moving average of the last five years of the term price, not today's spot. That is also why the term line above matters far more than the spot line.

§ 02 — Supply

Ninety per cent, and inflating

In 2024, the last year with a verified global total, the world's mines produced 60,213 tU against reactor requirements of roughly 68,920 tU. Mine supply covered 90% of consumption — up from 78% in 2022, which is the direction bulls point to. The problem is what it cost to get there.

Non-obvious angle n°3

The lowest-cost producer on earth is inflating at 30% a year

Kazatomprom mines uranium by in-situ recovery — pumping acid into a sandstone orebody and pumping uranium back out. It is structurally the cheapest way to produce a pound, and Kazatomprom's FY2025 C1 cash cost was US$18.06/lb. Its own 2026 guidance is US$23.50–25.00/lb. All-in sustaining cost guidance is US$35.00–36.50/lb against an FY2025 actual of US$29.53.

Three drivers, all disclosed. Sulphuric acid — the core reagent — rose 42.6% in 2025 to KZT 70,092 per tonne. The mineral extraction tax went from 6% to 9% on 1 January 2025 and became a differentiated rate on 1 January 2026, with the company's own sensitivity table showing effective rates of 11.2% to 14.3%. And in August 2025 the company cut 2026 nominal capacity from 32,777 tU to 29,697 tU following amendments to its subsoil use agreements — roughly 8 million pounds, described by the company as "about 5% of the world's primary supply".

The read-through is uncomfortable for the bull case: if the marginal cost of the cheapest pounds on the planet is rising 20–38% a year, the incentive price for the expensive pounds that would actually close a structural deficit is rising faster still. That is not a bearish observation. It is an argument that the price needs to go higher than most decks assume — and that the operating leverage everyone models is smaller than they think.

Non-obvious angle n°4 · a warning about every cost curve you have seen

The uranium cost curve is a category error. Every deck in this sector plots a bar chart of "cost per pound" across companies. Almost all of them are comparing metrics that are not the same metric.

Kazatomprom publishes C1 and AISC. Paladin publishes only C1 and explicitly does not disclose AISC. Boss Energy publishes C1 and AISC in Australian dollars. Ur-Energy publishes cash cost, non-cash cost and total cost per pound sold. Uranium Energy Corp publishes cash cost and total cost. Cameco guides an "average unit cost of sales including depreciation and amortisation" — a fundamentally different animal that includes purchased material. NexGen publishes a life-of-mine operating cost and no AISC at all. Energy Fuels quotes a mining-plus-milling figure for one mine.

Putting those on one axis produces a chart where Cameco looks three times more expensive than Kazatomprom, which is an artefact of accounting, not geology. § 03 — Demand

The AI story and the fuel cycle are two different stories

441 reactors operating, 404 GWe of capacity, 79 under construction and 123 planned. That is the real demand base, and it is growing — China alone has approved roughly ten units a year for three consecutive years. What it is not is the story being sold to equity investors, which is that hyperscaler data centres are about to consume uranium. They are not, and the arithmetic is not close.

Non-obvious angle n°5

Thirteen gigawatts of announcements, roughly two of incremental fuel demand

The Carnegie Endowment published the only rigorous public accounting of hyperscaler nuclear commitments in June 2026. Its conclusion: up to 13 GW announced, of which about 6.9 GW sits in power purchase agreements landing by the early 2030s, and about 6.1 GW sits in direct partnerships that are "contingent on technologies that have not yet been commercialized." If every project materialised, the total would generate around 102 TWh a year — covering, in Carnegie's words, "less than 20 percent of projected demand through 2035."

Now apply the fuel-cycle filter, which nobody does. A twenty-year PPA between Meta and Vistra for 2,176 MW from Perry and Davis-Besse is a genuinely excellent deal for Vistra. Those reactors are already running. They already buy uranium. The PPA creates precisely zero incremental pounds of demand. The same is true of Meta–Constellation at Clinton, Amazon–Talen at Susquehanna, Amazon–Vistra at Comanche Peak and Constellation's 920 MW of undisclosed new contracts.

What does create incremental demand is new fuel loaded into reactors that were not previously running. In the next five years, that is three US restarts: Palisades (800 MW), Crane (835 MW) and Duane Arnold (615 MW). About 2,250 MW. And as of this data cut, not one of them has produced a single megawatt-hour. Palisades — fuel on site since October 2025 — is roughly six months past its own deadline, with 1,400 cracked steam generator tubes sleeved and over 5,000 individual work activities still open. Crane's operating licence decision is expected May 2027. Duane Arnold targets Q1 2029.

Uprates, the other lever, add 433 MW under the Meta–Vistra deal — arriving 2031 through 2034, outside the window. For scale: cumulative US uprates from 1977 to 2021 totalled 8,030 MW, an average of 0.18 GW a year over 44 years.

Contracting: the number that actually predicts the price

Utilities do not buy uranium continuously. They contract in waves, and the volume of long-term contracting is the single best leading indicator in this sector — better than spot, better than inventory. The theoretical replacement rate, the volume needed simply to cover annual consumption, is roughly 150 million pounds a year. Utilities have contracted below that for thirteen consecutive years.

§ 04 — The bottleneck

1 January 2028

Public Law 118-62, the Prohibiting Russian Uranium Imports Act, was signed on 13 May 2024. The import ban took effect on 11 August 2024, subject to waivers issued by the Secretary of Energy under a shrinking annual quota. The statute contains one sentence that matters more than any price forecast in this report: "Any waiver issued under subparagraph (A) shall terminate not later than January 1, 2028."

There is no discretion in that sentence. From 1 January 2028 there is no legal route for Russian low-enriched uranium into the United States, regardless of whether an alternative source exists, until the prohibition itself sunsets in 2040. The quotas step down to the cliff: 476,536 kg in 2024, 470,376 kg in 2025, 464,183 kg in 2026, 459,083 kg in 2027, and then nothing.

Non-obvious angle n°6

Every gram of new Western enrichment arrives after the deadline

The Western response to the 2028 cliff has been substantial in dollars and almost entirely absent in timing. Every announced project lands after the waiver terminates, and most of it lands after 2030.

Urenco USA is completing +700,000 SWU in early 2027 — the only meaningful capacity that arrives before the cliff — and then announced a further +2.1 million SWU in June 2026 with initial cascades producing in 2032 and the balance through 2036. Orano's Georges Besse II extension, a €1.7bn programme adding roughly 30%, comes online progressively from 2028 with full commissioning by 2030. Orano's US "Project Ike", up to 7.5 million SWU, ramps from 2031. Centrus began commercial enrichment activities at Piketon in December 2025 with first new capacity guided to 2029. Urenco's UK high-assay facility starts construction in 2028 with first product in the early 2030s.

The 2028–2031 window is therefore structurally short of Western separative work by construction, not by accident. Whether that produces a price spike, a scramble for non-Russian tails, or a quiet extension of the deadline by another means is the single most important open question in this sector — and it is a legal and political question, not a geological one.

The counter-argument, stated fairly

Enrichment is not the same as uranium, and a Western SWU shortage does not automatically mean a uranium shortage. If separative work is scarce and expensive, enrichers overfeed — using more natural uranium per unit of product — which is bullish uranium. But utilities can also simply buy Russian-origin enriched product delivered through third countries where legal, draw down the very large existing inventories (US civil stockpiles alone were 42,000 tU at end-2024), or accept higher fuel costs, which are a small fraction of a nuclear plant's operating economics. The cliff is real and the date is fixed. The mechanism by which it transmits to the uranium price is genuinely uncertain, and anyone who tells you otherwise is guessing.

§ 05 — The ticker book

Nineteen names, five roles

The sector is routinely traded as one thing. It is five. A pound of uranium travels from a mine, to a converter, to an enricher, to a fuel fabricator, to a reactor operator — and the economics, the cyclicality and the correct valuation method are different at every step. A company that mines has operating leverage to the commodity. A company that enriches has a monopoly-adjacent tolling margin. A company that operates reactors is a utility whose fuel cost is a rounding error. Owning all three is not diversification.

What actually happened in Q2. Net earnings fell 92%. Almost all of it is one item: the prior-year quarter contained Westinghouse's Dukovany contract, worth roughly US$170m to Cameco's 49% share. Strip that and the underlying uranium business is fine — H1 uranium gross profit of C$417m against C$420m, with a realised price up from US$59.66 to US$66.96. Production guidance of 19.5–21.5 Mlb was held despite spring flooding at McArthur River and a two-week Cigar Lake suspension in July caused by problems at Orano's McClean Lake sulphuric acid plant.

The Westinghouse accounting trap. This is where most write-ups go wrong in one direction or the other. Cameco equity-accounts its 49% stake rather than consolidating it. The 2023 acquisition allocated a very large amount to intangibles, so Cameco's share of Westinghouse depreciation and amortisation is guided at US$275–290m for 2026, plus US$120–135m of finance costs. Against guided adjusted EBITDA of US$370–430m, that mechanically produces a guided net loss of US$10–75m — on a business that is cash-generative and, per guidance, running at roughly 20% historic margins. Quote the EBITDA and you overstate it. Quote the net loss and you understate it. Both numbers are in the same table of the same document.

The government partnership. Announced 28 October 2025 with Brookfield and the US government: "at least $80 billion of new reactors" built on Westinghouse technology. The government takes 20% of Westinghouse cash distributions above US$17.5bn, and if it invests by January 2029 and an IPO occurs at a US$30bn-or-higher valuation, it may require the IPO and convert into a warrant for roughly 20% of public value. In June 2026 the Department of Energy added a conditional commitment of up to US$17.5bn for long-lead components on up to ten AP1000s. Cameco's own filing does not disclose Westinghouse's enterprise value, the carrying value of its 49% stake, or the capital contributions involved, and flags in its risk language "the inability of Westinghouse and the US Government to enter into definitive agreements."

The position, honestly. Cameco is 22.41% of the Global X Uranium ETF, 19.65% of Sprott Uranium Miners and 7.83% of VanEck Uranium & Nuclear. It is the sector's index. It also trades at 167 times trailing earnings on a quarter with C$25m of net income and a Westinghouse equity loss. The bull case is genuinely strong — tier-one assets, a contract book averaging over 28 Mlb of annual deliveries for five years, C$3.1bn of liquidity, and the only Western reactor technology franchise of scale. The bear case is not about the business. It is that the entire passive complex owns it at the same multiple at the same time.

Non-obvious angle n°7

The largest discretionary buyer of physical uranium has switched itself off

The Sprott Physical Uranium Trust holds 81,697,348 lb of U₃O₈ — a net asset value of US$7.1bn — and it is the single most important price-setting entity in the spot market. Its mechanism is simple: when the units trade at a premium to net asset value, it issues units through an at-the-market programme and uses the proceeds to buy physical uranium. That is what produced the January 2026 spike, when a US$214m raise funded a 500,000 lb purchase and spot broke $100 on a daily basis.

As of 5 August 2026 the trust trades at a −10.30% discount to net asset value, described elsewhere as a 15-month wide and having gone from −9.0% to −12.3% in a single month. A trust at a discount cannot issue units accretively. The at-the-market programme is therefore effectively closed, and the marginal discretionary bid in the spot market is gone. Yellow Cake plc, the smaller listed vehicle holding 23.2 million pounds, sits at a −13.4% discount and added nothing in July.

This single mechanism explains a large share of why spot has drifted down all year while term rose. It also means that the flow story is now entirely a utility-contracting story. Watch the discount, not the holdings.

§ 08 — The critical-minerals adjacency

The strategic-minerals basket is mostly a narrative

Uranium and rare earths are routinely traded and written about as one "energy security" theme. We tested that claim the only way that produces evidence rather than opinion: by reading the actual constituent files of every relevant exchange-traded fund. The result does not support the narrative.

Non-obvious angle n°8

The three major uranium funds hold zero pure rare-earth equities. Zero.

Not MP Materials. Not Lynas. Not USA Rare Earth. Not United States Antimony. Across URA, URNM and NLR — $11.9bn of combined assets — the only bridge between the two sectors is Energy Fuels, held at 3.19–4.22% and classified in every case as a uranium miner. It happens to produce neodymium-praseodymium at White Mesa, which is why it is the sole genuine two-sided exposure in the listed universe.

The overlap is not reciprocal either. REMX, the main rare-earth fund, holds zero uranium names. Exactly one product deliberately bundles both: Sprott's Critical Materials fund, at 25.19% uranium and 11.40% rare earths — a real index-construction argument, but with 158 holdings and a small fraction of URA's assets, its flow influence is marginal.

The fundamentals argue against a single factor too. Year to date in 2026: MP Materials −5.0%, USA Rare Earth +44.2%, Lynas +33.7%, United States Antimony +27.6%. Their input prices diverged as sharply — dysprosium and terbium roughly doubled while antimony fell. That dispersion is not what a co-moving basket looks like. We found no analyst note, correlation coefficient or beta study quantifying uranium versus rare-earth equity co-movement. Treat the claim as unsourced until someone produces one.

That said, the sector is genuinely relevant to a uranium investor for one reason: it is the live experiment in whether Western governments will underwrite critical-minerals economics directly. And here the honest count is small.

§ 09 — Outlook

What is actually knowable

Forecasting a commodity price is not a differentiated activity. Identifying which dates are fixed, which are contingent and which are aspirational is. Below, the calendar sorted by how much it can slip.

Fixed by statute or by a signed licence

  • 1 January 2028 — every Russian uranium import waiver terminates. Written into Public Law 118-62. Not subject to Department of Energy discretion. The prohibition itself runs to 31 December 2040.
  • 31 March 2036 — NexGen's construction licence expires. Rook I must be built inside that window.
  • Annual waiver quotas step down to 459,083 kg in 2027, then zero.

The next twelve months — dated and checkable

  • 7 August 2026 — Vistra Q2. 21 August — Kazatomprom H1 financials, with the company having explicitly reserved the right to revise revenue, C1, AISC and capex guidance, and historically the first read on next-year production plans. End August — Boss Energy's updated Honeymoon feasibility study, life-of-mine plan and resource; and the expected close of Energy Fuels' ASM acquisition.
  • September 2026 — final environmental determination for the Crane restart. 30 September — Centrus's HALEU transition period ends.
  • Q3–Q4 2026 — Bannerman's CNNC investment completion and Etango final investment decision; Deep Yellow's Tumas decision, deferred once already; the expiry of China's one-year suspension of its October 2025 rare-earth export controls, around November.
  • Through 2027 — Crane operating licence decision expected May; Palisades restart, whenever it comes; Urenco USA's +700,000 SWU completing early in the year; Paladin's PLS hearings and final investment decision targeted at year end.

Two to three years — 2028 to 2029

This is the window the entire report points at. Four things converge. The Russian waiver cliff hits on 1 January 2028 with essentially no new Western separative work in place. Orano's Georges Besse II extension begins coming online through 2030, and Centrus's first new capacity is guided to 2029 — both after the cliff. Denison's Phoenix targets first production mid-2028, the first genuinely new Western supply of scale in a decade. And Cameco's own disclosure notes that its contract commitments are above average in 2026–2028 and below average in 2029–2030 — meaning the sector's largest producer has a re-contracting event at exactly the moment the term price is at an eighteen-year high.

The plausible shape: fuel-cycle bottleneck pricing dominates, enrichment and conversion re-rate ahead of uranium, and the producers with unencumbered or market-related contract books capture the term price while legacy books continue to drag. The observable that would confirm it is not the spot price. It is annual long-term contracting volume crossing back above 150 million pounds. It has not done so in thirteen years.

Five to ten years — 2031 to 2036

Beyond about three years, the honest position is that specific forecasts are storytelling. What can be said is which structural facts are already locked in:

  • The 2040 arithmetic is not close. The World Nuclear Association's reference scenario requires roughly 150,000 tU a year by 2040 against 2024 production of 60,213 tU. Even the lower scenario needs 107,000 tU. The Association's own language is that "existing mines face a depletion of resources in the middle of the next decade" — the base has to be replaced before it can be grown.
  • Project timelines have lengthened, not shortened. The industry's own estimate, given at the 2025 report launch: from "8 to 15 years" historically to "between 10 to 20 years" now. A mine started today does not help before 2036.
  • Small modular reactors consume almost nothing until the 2030s and then need a fuel that barely exists. Cumulative Western high-assay production is under two tonnes against a projected US requirement of roughly fifty tonnes a year by 2035. Every advanced-reactor valuation embeds a solution to that which has not been built.
  • China is the swing consumer and is resource-poor. Roughly ten reactor approvals a year for three straight years — eleven in 2024, ten in 2025, eight in July 2026 — against a target of 200 GWe by 2035. China's own uranium production is small. That demand has to be sourced externally, and the December 2025 Kazakh subsoil amendments make its access to Kazakh ounces structurally harder.
  • The secondary supply cushion is disappearing on schedule. The Megatons to Megawatts programme ended in 2013. Total secondary supply was around 9,000 tU in 2021 and is expected below 5,000 tU by 2030. That is a known, dated decline that nothing can reverse.

What we will not do is put a price on that. The bracket the sourced evidence supports for a greenfield incentive price is roughly $95–115/lb for tier-one projects with completed studies — NexGen's own $95 base case at the low end, Cameco's disclosed market-related contract midpoints of $100–115 at the high end. Beyond that bracket, the marginal projects required to close a 200-million-pound structural gap need materially more, and nobody has published a defensible number for how much.

§ 10 — What would break this

The bear case, argued properly

A research note that cannot state its own falsification conditions is marketing. Here are the five things that would invalidate most of what is above, with the evidence for each.

1. Kazatomprom restores volume

The 2026 capacity cut removed roughly 8 million pounds — about 5% of world primary supply — and it is discretionary and reversible. Kazatomprom is currently realising $67.88/lb against a term price near $96. As legacy contracts roll off, the incentive to restore subsoil-agreement volumes grows. Into a market that contracted 33 million pounds in the first half of 2026, 8 million pounds of returning supply is not marginal. Watch the 21 August guidance update.

2. Utilities simply do not come back

Thirteen consecutive years below replacement-rate contracting can be read two ways. Bulls call it stored demand. The bearish reading is that utilities have adequate inventory, are comfortable, and have demonstrated for over a decade that they can run without contracting at replacement rate. US civil stockpiles alone were 42,000 tU at end-2024, with 40,000 tU in the EU and 65,000 tU in East Asia. That is several years of consumption sitting in warehouses. Sprott's July 2026 note states 2026 term volumes remain "well below the replacement rate."

3. The restart and small-modular narrative keeps slipping

Palisades has now missed December 2025, "early 2026" and end-February 2026, with 1,400 cracked steam generator tubes and over 5,000 open work activities as of July. Crane's licence decision is expected May 2027. Duane Arnold targets 2029. NuScale has an approved design and no signed offtake. Oklo's first unit proceeds under Department of Energy authorisation because its Nuclear Regulatory Commission application was denied in 2022. Every one of those slipping another year pushes incremental fuel demand out of the modellable window — and the equity market has already started repricing exactly that, which is what §07 shows.

4. The historical precedent is brutal and long

Uranium peaked at roughly $136/lb in June 2007 having started that year at $72. It was below $50 by early 2009 and below $18 by 2016 — an 87% decline over nine years. After Fukushima, URA fell more than 58% in 2011 alone, and USEC — the direct predecessor of today's Centrus — fell 79% that year with short interest peaking at 26% of shares outstanding in July. URA's since-inception annualised return, fifteen years on, is still −2.51%. This sector has destroyed more capital than it has created, over the full cycle, for investors who bought conviction at the top.

5. Valuation is stretched in the places nobody checks

The most extreme absolute multiples in this sector are not in the small modular reactor names — those have no earnings to divide by. They are in the fuel cycle. Cameco trades at 167× trailing earnings, on a quarter with C$25m of net income and a Westinghouse equity loss, while being the largest position in every major fund. Centrus trades at 89× trailing and 176× EV/EBITDA. If either of those multiples normalises toward the sector's own history, no supply-deficit narrative saves the equity.

The reflexive risk in the fuel-cycle thesis itself

The enrichment argument in §04 has a genuine counter that deserves stating. A Western separative-work shortage does not mechanically become a uranium shortage. Utilities can buy enriched product of Russian origin through third countries where that remains legal, they can draw the very large existing inventories, and — critically — fuel is a small share of a nuclear plant's operating cost, so higher fuel prices are absorbable rather than demand-destroying. The 1 January 2028 date is fixed. The transmission mechanism from that date to the uranium price is not, and this report does not claim it is.

Three things to get right before this goes near a published page

Currency. KAP.L returns pence, not pounds and not dollars. PDN.AX and BOE.AX return Australian dollars. Rendering all of them with a dollar sign is the most common error in retail dashboards and it is off by a factor of roughly 100 on the London line. Always read q.currency and label accordingly.

Licensing. Yahoo's data is not licensed for commercial redistribution. For a public research site that carries advertising or a subscription, use a licensed vendor. For a personal site, at minimum attribute the source and state the delay.

Never let a live price update a fundamental. The temptation is to compute a "gap to target" or a live price-to-earnings ratio. Do not. Everything in this report other than the price column is a filed figure with a stated period. Mixing a real-time quote into a table of quarterly disclosures produces numbers that look precise and are not. Keep the live column clearly separated and clearly labelled with its own timestamp.

§ 12 — Glossary

Plain definitions

U₃O₈ / yellowcake
Uranium oxide concentrate — what a mine actually ships. Priced in US dollars per pound. It is not fuel; it is three processing steps away from being fuel.
Conversion
Turning U₃O₈ into uranium hexafluoride gas so it can be enriched. Priced per kilogram of uranium. Five plants in the world do it at scale.
SWU — separative work unit
The measure of effort required to enrich uranium. Not a quantity of material, a quantity of work. Priced per SWU.
Enrichment
Raising the concentration of the fissile isotope U-235 from 0.7% in nature to 3–5% for a conventional reactor. Four organisations control over 99% of world capacity.
HALEU
High-assay low-enriched uranium — enriched to just under 20%, required by almost every advanced reactor design. One Western commercial producer exists.
Underfeeding / overfeeding
An enricher's choice to use more separative work and less uranium, or the reverse. Purely a function of relative prices. Overfeeding removes secondary supply and increases uranium demand.
Spot vs term price
Spot is a pound today in a thin market. Term is the indicative price for a multi-year delivery contract signed today. Utilities buy the vast majority of their fuel on term.
Realised price
What a producer actually banked per pound, after its existing contract book. Typically 15–25% below the market price. This is the number that drives earnings.
C1 cash cost
The direct cash cost of producing a pound. Excludes sustaining capital, corporate overhead, royalties in some definitions, and depreciation always.
AISC
All-in sustaining cost — C1 plus the capital needed to keep production flat. The honest measure of whether a mine funds itself. Several producers in this report do not disclose it.
ISR — in-situ recovery
Dissolving uranium underground with acid or alkaline solution and pumping it up, rather than mining rock. Cheaper and lower impact — but recovery rates are modelled, not measured, until a wellfield is leached.
NPV₈ / IRR
Net present value at an 8% discount rate, and internal rate of return. Both are entirely dependent on the uranium price assumed — always check the price deck before comparing two projects.
Price deck
The uranium price a feasibility study assumes. Denison assumes $68.89–78.36. NexGen assumes a flat $95. Two projects are not comparable unless you know both decks.
Backlog
Contracted future revenue. Note that Centrus's $4.5bn "backlog" and the much smaller ASC 606 "remaining performance obligations" in the same filing are different measures. Do not conflate them.
Equity accounting
Reporting a minority stake as a single share-of-profit line rather than consolidating it. Cameco's 49% of Westinghouse is equity-accounted, which is why its EBITDA share and its net earnings share tell opposite stories.
Discount to NAV
A closed-end fund trading below the value of what it owns. For the Sprott physical trust this is functionally an on/off switch for the fund's uranium buying.
