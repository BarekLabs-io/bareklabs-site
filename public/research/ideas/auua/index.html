§ 00What this report is, and what it will not do

ALUULA Composites is a C$114 million company with C$10.4 million of trailing revenue, no analyst coverage, no published guidance, roughly C$45,000 of daily trading volume, and a share price that has risen 271% in twelve months. It is also, on the numbers it does publish, one of the cleanest small-cap growth records on the TSX Venture Exchange: revenue up 90% in the first half of its fiscal 2026, gross margin steady in the low forties for six consecutive quarters, and a balance sheet that went from C$2.1 million of cash to C$13.7 million in a single quarter.

Both of those sentences are true at once. This report is an attempt to hold them together honestly.

The thesis under examination is that ALUULA is extending from sporting goods into defence, military and aerospace end markets. We take that thesis seriously and test it against what the company has actually filed and announced. The short version, developed in §08: the defence narrative is real as a direction and almost entirely absent as a business. There is one defence-framed announcement, zero defence contracts, zero disclosed defence revenue, and two statutory barriers — the US Berry Amendment and the EU SAFE 35% non-European input cap — that structurally exclude a Canadian-made laminate from the two largest defence procurement pools on earth. What remains addressable is Canada's own C$180 billion, ten-year procurement plan, plus non-SAFE European and commercial-adjacent demand. That is not nothing. It is also not what the word "defence" implies to most readers.

Scope

What this report will not do

  • It will not print an EBITDA figure for ALUULA. The company has never published EBITDA or adjusted EBITDA in any MD&A, press release or audited statement. Its MD&A does contain a subtotal called "income before interest, tax, amortization", which looks like EBITDA and is not — it is struck after share-based compensation and before other income. Any EBITDA number you see attributed to ALUULA was computed by a data vendor or invented.
  • It will not print a free cash flow figure. No FCF definition or figure exists in any filing.
  • It will not issue a price target. No broker covers this stock; there is no consensus to anchor to, and on C$45,000 of daily liquidity a target is a fiction with decimal places.
  • It will not size the defence opportunity in dollars. ALUULA has disclosed no defence pipeline value, no named prospect and no stage detail. Anyone quoting one is asserting, not citing.
  • It will not use ALUULA's own "US$60bn global composites market" framing. That number appears in the company's investor deck without an external citation and describes the entire composites industry — aerospace prepreg, wind blades, carbon fibre. It is the wrong denominator for a 17-to-220 gram-per-square-metre flexible laminate business by roughly two orders of magnitude.

Everything below is sourced to a filing, a press release, a regulator or a named market study. Where a study is a paid consultant report, it is flagged as such — those numbers routinely disagree by two to five times between houses and should be read as directional. Where a number does not exist, the report says so rather than filling the gap.

§ 01What ALUULA actually sells

ALUULA makes flexible composite laminates from ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene — the same polymer family that underlies Dyneema and Spectra. The material is roughly eight times stronger than steel for equivalent weight, does not absorb water, resists UV, and is chemically inert.

The differentiating claim is not the fibre. It is the joining method. Conventional UHMWPE laminates — Dyneema Composite Fabric being the reference product — bond a fibre grid between polyester films using an adhesive. ALUULA instead fuses a mono-polymer stack: everything in the laminate is polyethylene, joined at the molecular level without glue. Three consequences follow, and they are the entire technical case:

  • No delamination. There is no adhesive layer to fail, which matters most in saltwater, UV and repeated flex-cycling — kites, sails, towing kites, inflatable structures.
  • Weldable, not sewn. A mono-polymer thermoplastic can be heat-welded and thermoformed, and field-repaired with a heat-activated patch. That removes stitch holes, which are the failure point in any pressure-holding or waterproof structure.
  • Recycle-ready. A single-polymer laminate can be chemically recycled; a film-adhesive-fibre sandwich effectively cannot. Note the caveat the company itself publishes: the original ALUULA Gold 82 and Gold 124 grades are not recyclable. The next-generation grades are.

Product families: ALUULA Gold (the original windsport material), DURLYTE (windsport line), GRAFLYTE (the graphene-containing outdoor/pack line). Areal densities run from 25–35 grams per square metre at the ultralight end — tents, sails, outerwear — to 155–220 gsm for rafts, sails and airships. The company's own product table maps that heaviest band to "durable shelters."

Published, and not published

The material data gap

ALUULA publishes areal densities, qualitative strength claims and application mappings. It publishes no absolute engineering values — no tensile strength in newtons, no tear resistance in kilonewtons per metre, no burst pressure, no hydrostatic head. It publishes no ballistic data of any kind: no V50, no NIJ level, no backface deformation, no areal-density-versus-threat curve. It publishes no dielectric or RF data, which is what a radome conversation would require.

This is normal for a consumer-materials supplier and disqualifying for a defence one. It is the single most concrete measure of how far the company is from a protective-equipment sale, and it is discussed again in §08.

§ 02The revenue record

ALUULA's fiscal year ends 31 October. This trips people up constantly: "Q2 fiscal 2026" is the quarter ended 30 April 2026, reported on 24 June. Q3 FY2026 closed on 31 July 2026 and has not yet been reported. Every reference below is to fiscal periods.

Data hazard

The FY2023 restatement trap

ALUULA acquired Ocean Rodeo, a kiteboarding brand, on 31 October 2022 and discontinued it on 29 April 2024. FY2023 was subsequently restated to continuing operations only. Reported FY2023 revenue is therefore either C$7,347,300 or C$4,161,686 depending on which filing you open. Any growth rate computed across that boundary — and several third-party sites still do — is wrong. Every figure in this report uses the restated continuing-operations basis for FY2023.

The record in plain terms. FY2022 C$2.74M. FY2023 C$4.16M restated. FY2024 C$6.36M, up 53%. FY2025 C$7.87M, up 24% — a visible deceleration. Then the reacceleration: Q1 FY2026 C$2,330,926, up 85%; Q2 FY2026 C$3,056,473, up 94%. First-half revenue of C$5,387,399 against C$2,835,206, up 90%. Trailing twelve months through 30 April: C$10,425,414.

The FY2025 slowdown matters for reading the current numbers. It was not a demand problem in the abstract — it was a company whose revenue was, in its own words, "primarily (circa 70%+) related to Gold materials from windsport customers" running into the natural ceiling of a single niche. What changed in FY2026 is mix, and mix is the subject of §06.

§ 03Margin is holding. The cost line is not.

The single most impressive operating fact about ALUULA is gross margin stability. The company states a target band of 40–45% and has now printed inside it for six consecutive quarters, through a period in which quarterly revenue more than doubled and product mix shifted materially. Materials businesses do not usually manage that during a scale-up.

The cost line is the other half of the picture, and it is going the wrong way.

Non-obvious angle n°1

Operating leverage was spent, not lost

Q4 FY2025 was the only profitable quarter in ALUULA's history: net comprehensive income of C$77,742 on revenue of C$2,916,427. It happened because full-year FY2025 operating expenses were C$4,330,832 — and excluding share-based compensation, only C$3,622,090, actually below FY2024's C$4,554,341. The company had cut costs while revenue rose. That is what produced the profit.

Then, in the two quarters after that profit, opex ran at an annualised C$6.0 million. Headcount in production rose 54% in Q1 FY2026 alone. A CFO was hired at a base sufficient to warrant 308,919 options. An R&D facility was opened in Squamish. A Vancouver plant lease was signed.

The company did not lose its operating leverage — it deliberately spent it, in the same quarter it raised C$14.1 million, in order to buy capacity it does not yet have. Whether that was correct is the whole investment question. But it means the reported loss trend in FY2026 tells you almost nothing about the underlying unit economics, and the FY2025 gross-margin-times-frozen-cost-base experiment tells you a great deal: this business model reaches profitability at a revenue level it can plausibly reach.

The arithmetic is uncomfortable but not alarming. At the current 43% margin and a C$6.6 million cost base, ALUULA needs roughly C$15.3 million of annual revenue to break even at the operating line — around C$3.8 million a quarter, or about 25% above the Q2 FY2026 print. If the Vancouver facility pushes the cost base to C$9 million, breakeven moves to C$20.9 million. At C$11.5 million of opex, C$26.7 million. Every one of those numbers is achievable at the capacity the company says it is building. None of them is achievable at the capacity it has today.

§ 04The capacity ceiling is the whole story

ALUULA's Victoria plant runs two "fusion pods." Each is rated at roughly 26,000 square metres of Gold material per month on a standard work week with twelve-hour shifts, staffed by two to three people. Each pod has a five-year useful life and, as of 31 October 2025, about two years of that remaining.

As of Q2 FY2026 the company states Victoria is at 100% utilisation. The December 2025 shareholder letter had already warned that Q4 FY2025's C$2.9 million "tested the limits of current production capacity."

This produces an unusual situation. For the next two quarters, ALUULA's revenue is not a demand variable. It is a physics variable. The plant makes what the plant makes. Q3 and Q4 FY2026 will land near C$3 million each not because that is what customers ordered but because that is what two pods produce. Which is why the most important line in the Q2 filing is not the revenue number at all.

Non-obvious angle n°2

A falling backlog at 100% utilisation has two opposite meanings

The order book fell from C$3.5 million at 31 January 2026 to C$2.7 million at 30 April 2026. In a normal company that is a demand warning. In a plant running flat out it is genuinely ambiguous, and the two readings point in opposite directions:

Reading one, benign. Backlog is the queue. If throughput improves — and production headcount rose 54% in Q1 — the queue drains faster than it fills, and a falling backlog is a sign the plant is catching up with its customers. This is what you want to see before a capacity expansion, because it means the new plant will not simply inherit an unserved order book.

Reading two, adverse. The company disclosed more than C$6 million of binding purchase agreements at 31 October 2025, of which over C$5 million was planned for first-half FY2026 fulfilment. First-half revenue was C$5.39 million. Those agreements were therefore substantially consumed. If the backlog is now C$2.7 million and no comparable binding-agreement disclosure has replaced the C$6 million figure, the forward book is thinner than it was a year ago — at exactly the moment the company is committing C$4 million of capex to a ten-fold capacity expansion.

ALUULA has not said which reading is correct, and the distinction is not disclosed anywhere. It is the single highest-value question to put to management, and the Q3 FY2026 report — due by roughly 29 September 2026 — is the first opportunity to resolve it. A backlog that recovers above C$3.5M validates reading one. A second sequential decline validates reading two, and would matter far more than whatever the revenue line prints.

The Vancouver build

The expansion is concrete and dated, which is more than most microcap capacity stories offer. Lease signed, disclosed as a subsequent event to Q2 FY2026. Possession October 2026. Target operational "end of this fiscal" — 31 October 2026. C$4.0 million of the February placement was allocated to manufacturing capacity, plus C$2.6 million of working capital for the new facility. The headline capability is roll width: 1.5 metres against Victoria's 0.925 metres.

Not disclosed

Total Vancouver square footage, lease cost, total project capex beyond the C$4.0M allocation, the number of fusion pods planned, and the capacity target in revenue rather than multiple terms. Actual capex in the first half of FY2026 was only C$314,124 — meaning as of 30 April, essentially none of the C$4.0M had been spent. The build is almost entirely ahead of the company, not behind it.

§ 05The width trade

Everything the company wants to do next — shelters, structural panels, sails, defence, aerospace — is gated by a single number that appears nowhere in the financial statements: how wide the roll is.

Non-obvious angle n°3

The binding constraint is not demand, and it is not capital. It is 128 millimetres.

A 0.925-metre roll is a serious commercial handicap in technical textiles, for a reason that has nothing to do with material performance. Patterns for tents, shelters, sails, packs and panels are laid out for standard-width goods. Cut a 1.5-metre pattern from a 0.925-metre roll and you either seam it — introducing exactly the stitch line the mono-polymer weld was supposed to eliminate — or you waste material. ALUULA states the 1.5-metre width delivers roughly 15% better material yield for customers. For a laminate at the price point of a UHMWPE composite, a 15% yield gain is not a feature. It is a change in the landed cost of the finished product.

It also explains the sequencing of every announcement the company has made. The first 1.5-metre material shipped to tent brands in October 2025 — before the financing, before the Vancouver lease. That was a capability demonstration, not a product launch. The February 2026 raise funded the plant that makes it routine. The company's own investor materials state plainly that the 1.5-metre width is what "opens commercial shipping, defence, and aerospace" as markets.

So the honest framing of this equity is not "a defence materials company." It is: a company that spent C$14 million to buy 128 millimetres of roll width, betting that width is what converts a windsport niche into an industrial materials business. If the Vancouver plant lands on time at 1.5 metres, the addressable conversation changes. If it slips, nothing else in the thesis matters, because the products that need the width cannot be quoted at all.

This is also the cleanest thing to monitor. It is binary, it is dated, and it does not require you to trust a pipeline claim: possession October 2026, operational by 31 October 2026.

§ 06Who actually buys, and how concentrated it is

The mix migration is real and fast. Pack and bag customers went from one to seven in Q1 FY2026 alone, a 573% year-on-year revenue increase in that channel. Commercial industrial grew 162%. Windsport grew 26% — healthy, but a fraction of the rest. Twelve new customers were added in Q2. The company claims 100+ active customers across four development stages and 20 brands with products in market.

Named partners across the disclosed relationships: Arc'teryx (Alpha SL pack), Michelin Inflatable Solutions, Duotone Sports, Oceanicwing SAS / AirSeas, Aqua Dynamics, Hyperlite Mountain Gear, Mountain Hardwear, MAAP, Db Journey, Max Space.

Non-obvious angle n°4

The concentration disclosure measures the wrong thing

ALUULA's only hard concentration disclosure is in the credit-risk note of the FY2025 audited statements: "The Company's top two customers account for 75.5% of trade receivables at October 31, 2025, with the largest customer accounting for 39.5%."

Concentration as a percentage of revenue is not disclosed anywhere. Receivables concentration is a balance-sheet snapshot, not a revenue measure — it is distorted by payment terms, by timing, and by the fact that ageing was poor at the last disclosure (26% of receivables over 90 days at 31 July 2025, C$316,430).

Combine that with the geography chart and the ambiguity compounds. If revenue is booked to contract manufacturers, a single Western brand ordering through two factories appears as two customers. Conversely, several brands using the same Sri Lankan converter may appear as one. Neither the company's customer count of "100+" nor its receivables concentration tells you how many independent buying decisions this revenue actually represents. On a C$10 million revenue base with 75.5% of receivables in two names, the honest position is that concentration risk is high and imprecisely measurable — and that it is improving directionally, because the mix migration in the chart above is by definition a broadening of the base.

Non-obvious angle n°5

On this liquidity, the block prints are the only real price

ALUULA trades roughly 12,000 to 15,000 shares a day — about C$45,000 to C$55,000 of notional turnover against a C$114 million market capitalisation. A single institutional position of any conventional size cannot be built or exited through the order book at all. The float is 26.3 million shares and roughly a third of the register is insiders.

This has three practical consequences that no valuation model captures. First, the quoted price is not a clearing price for size — it is the marginal print of a very thin book, and it can move 10% on a few tens of thousands of dollars in either direction. Second, the only prices at which real size has actually changed hands are the two C$3.30 blocks: the February placement and the July founder sale. Both cleared arm's-length, five months apart, at an identical level, in a stock that quotes C$3.70. That is a meaningful data point about where informed size is willing to transact.

Third, and most important for position sizing: an illiquid stock with no analyst coverage and no guidance re-rates in both directions on flow, not on fundamentals. The 271% twelve-month gain and the C$1.16-to-C$4.86 52-week range are the same phenomenon as the risk of a 40% drawdown on a single disappointing quarter. Whatever view you take on the business, the position size has to be set by the exit, not the entry.

One nuance on the founder sale that cuts the other way: Richard Myerscough sold 645,000 shares off-market in a block described as following "continued institutional interest," and retains 5,000,256 shares — 16.18% — while remaining on the board. A founder reducing from 18.3% to 16.2% into institutional demand, after a six-fold re-rating, is ordinary financial planning. It would be a different signal at a different scale.

§ 08The defence extension, examined

This is the part of the story doing the most work in the share price and the least work in the income statement. Handled carefully, it is a legitimate medium-term optionality argument. Handled loosely, it is the kind of narrative that ends badly on a microcap.

What has actually been announced

The complete inventory, as of 6 August 2026, is one item.

23 April 2026

The Export Development Canada agreement

Following the insolvency of SRTX / Sheertex and its affiliate Octagon Performance Materials — a Notice of Intention filed 17 February 2026, PwC as proposal trustee — ALUULA "rehomed" select advanced manufacturing equipment for research into UHMWPE fibre, in an arrangement with Export Development Canada, Canada's export credit agency.

The release states that UHMWPE is used in "defence systems, ballistic protection, helmets, armour systems, and advanced composite structures for military platforms," and discloses the following: "Canada has reserved, for a period of three years, the right to access the specific equipment related to the production of UHMWPE fibres, now owned by ALUULA, if it is required for national security or defence."

The same release states that UHMWPE fibre production is not currently part of ALUULA's business plan. The consideration ALUULA paid, and any EDC financing, guarantee or grant amount, are not disclosed.

Three third parties are quoted supportively: Ray Castelli of Weatherhaven, Michael Smith of Unisync, and Guillermo Freire of EDC. None is described as a customer or partner. No agreement with either company was announced.

Non-obvious angle n°6

Canada did not place an order. ALUULA wrote Canada a call option.

The three-year access right is being read in some places as a government endorsement with commercial content. Structurally, it is the opposite: it is a contingent claim on ALUULA's asset, granted by ALUULA, exercisable at the Crown's discretion, with no disclosed strike, no disclosed compensation and no purchase obligation. Read it as a written option, not a received one.

That does not make it worthless. It is the clearest possible statement that Ottawa considers domestic UHMWPE fibre capability strategically relevant — and it arrived two months after Canada published its first Defence Industrial Strategy, whose entire framing is sovereign supply chains and "Buy Canadian." Being the company that holds the only Canadian UHMWPE fibre equipment, with a named relationship to EDC, is a genuine positioning asset in a procurement system that has just committed to awarding 70% of defence acquisitions to Canadian firms.

But the asset is equipment for a product line the company says it is not going to run. The honest description is: ALUULA acquired a strategic option on Canadian fibre sovereignty at an undisclosed price, in exchange for writing the Crown an access option on the same equipment. Both options are currently out of the money, and neither generates revenue.

What has not been announced

To be explicit about the largest absences. There is no defence contract. There is no MOU with any military, defence prime or government procurement body — not Canada's DND, not the US DoD, not NATO. There is no qualification or testing programme: no NIJ, no MIL-STD, no STANAG, no V50 ballistic testing, no first-article testing. There is no defence award from IDEaS in Canada, from DIU, SBIR or AFWERX in the United States, or from NATO DIANA. There is no defence-sector hire — no executive, no director and no advisor with a disclosed defence background. And there is no defence revenue disclosed in any filing, ever.

ALUULA's own investor deck is the fairest witness here. It classifies its markets into four maturity stages: Revenue-generating (wind sports), Scaling (technical outdoor), In development (commercial/industrial), and Pipeline. Defence — listed as "inflatables, shelters, UAVs" — and Aerospace — "structural composites" — are both in the Pipeline row. That is the company's own lowest stage. The word "UAVs" appearing in one cell of one slide is the entirety of ALUULA's public statement about drones.

Non-obvious angle n°7

The two biggest defence budgets on earth are closed by statute, not by competition

This is the part of the defence thesis that execution cannot fix, and it is almost never mentioned.

The United States. The Berry Amendment, 10 U.S.C. §4862, requires the Department of Defense to buy US-grown or US-produced food, clothing, and — critically — fibers, yarns and fabrics. A laminate produced in Victoria or Vancouver is not Berry-compliant for DoD textile procurement. This is not a preference or a tiebreaker; it is a statutory bar on the largest soldier-systems budget in the world. Note that Avient markets Dyneema Composite Fabric as "Proudly made in the USA" — the incumbent has already solved for exactly this. ALUULA has never publicly addressed Berry.

The European Union. The €150 billion SAFE instrument inside the €800 billion ReArm Europe / Readiness 2030 package requires that non-European inputs not exceed 35% of a procured system's value. A Canadian-made structural laminate counts against that ceiling. It is not excluded, but it is constrained — and it competes against European suppliers who are not.

What is left is real but much smaller. Canada's own programme: C$180 billion of procurement opportunities over ten years, C$290 billion of defence-related capital investment, an explicit 70%-to-Canadian-firms target, a C$4 billion BDC defence platform and a C$656.9 million defence technology commercialisation fund. Plus non-SAFE European procurement, national programmes outside the instrument, and commercial-adjacent buyers such as shelter and inflatables manufacturers who sell to defence but buy materials commercially.

That last channel — being a Tier-3 material inside a Canadian Tier-1's product — is by far the most plausible route, and it is exactly the shape of the Weatherhaven and Unisync quotations. Weatherhaven alone holds roughly C$168 million of DND shelter contracts, with options taking it toward C$350 million, for 1,435 tactical shelter systems. ALUULA has announced no relationship with Weatherhaven of any kind. But if a defence revenue line ever appears, this is the shape it will take: a few hundred thousand dollars of laminate inside somebody else's shelter, not a contract with a ministry.

Non-obvious angle n°8

The most valuable defence asset ALUULA owns is a board seat, and the announcement never mentions defence

On 17 March 2026, ALUULA appointed Christophe Dardel to its board, replacing the retiring Peter Berrang. Dardel is the former President of DSM Dyneema — the business that is now Avient Protective Materials, the owner of both the Dyneema fibre franchise and Dyneema Composite Fabric, and the single largest supplier of ballistic UHMWPE in the Western world. He also sat on the board of Nippon Dyneema, the DSM–Toyobo Japanese joint venture.

Read the appointment release and you will not find the word defence, ballistic, military or armour anywhere. ALUULA framed the hire entirely around "premium global ingredient brands and IP-rich materials platforms."

Two readings, and they are not exclusive. The generous one: the company recruited the person who has actually built and scaled the exact business ALUULA is trying to become, and understated it. The sceptical one: they recruited him for brand and channel strategy, and the defence relevance is an inference investors are making on their behalf.

Either way, this is the asset that would matter most if defence became real. Qualification programmes, prime relationships and ballistic testing pathways are relationship businesses with decade-long cycles, and ALUULA now has a director who has run one. It is also the reason to watch the board and executive announcements more closely than the press releases. The signal that would genuinely change this section is not another market-opportunity slide — it is a defence business development hire, a Controlled Goods Program registration, or the first published V50 test result. None has occurred.

Keep separate

Plausible narrative that ALUULA has never announced

Each of the following is a reasonable-sounding storyline constructible from the material properties and the macro backdrop, and none is announced, contracted or confirmed: that ALUULA is entering soft or hard body armour; that it has a drone or UAV customer; that it supplies Weatherhaven or Unisync; that it has a DND, DoD or NATO relationship; that the Crown's access right implies an order or a purchase option; that it will produce UHMWPE fibre; that Dardel was hired to open defence doors; that the Michelin shelter programme is a military programme; that Max Space is a defence programme; or that it is working on radomes, parachutes, aerial delivery, rigid-hull inflatable boats, naval sails, military load carriage or helmet shells. The last group has never been mentioned by the company at all.

§ 09Space, wind and shelters — the adjacencies that are real

Strip out defence and there are three non-consumer relationships with actual disclosed substance. They are smaller than the defence story and considerably more concrete.

Max Space — the only genuine aerospace revenue

ALUULA supplies UHMWPE composite laminate to Max Space for expandable space habitats. A habitat was displayed at the 39th Space Symposium in April 2024 and was scheduled to launch on SpaceX in 2026. This is commercial, not defence, and the contract value is not disclosed. It is nonetheless the only relationship in the portfolio where ALUULA material is a structural element in a pressure vessel — which is precisely the qualification-adjacent credential the shelter and inflatables markets ask for.

Michelin Inflatable Solutions — the shelter route

A collaboration launched 18 March 2024 completed its R&D phase on 9 October 2025 and moved to commercialisation, with first commercial product targeted for late 2025 and scale-up through 2026. The defence language in that release belongs to Michelin's heritage description of its own markets — "high-demanding applications such as Aeronautics & Space or Defense" — not to any ALUULA contract. Financial terms, licensing structure, volumes and revenue share: not disclosed.

This is the most likely first defence-adjacent revenue in the whole portfolio, because inflatable shelters are exactly where the 1.5-metre width, the weldability and the mono-polymer durability all pay off at once, and because Michelin sells into defence-facing customers without ALUULA having to hold any certification itself.

AirSeas / Oceanicwing — a binding contract with a hidden counterparty change

Announced 2 December 2025: a binding twelve-month supply purchase agreement effective 1 January 2026, with fixed pricing and minimum material purchase volumes. This is the only binding, volume-committed supply agreement ALUULA has disclosed with a named industrial counterparty. The application is the Seawing towing kite for cargo vessels: a 300 m² kite tested in June 2025, a 600 m² kite in testing targeting 150 km/h dynamic flight speeds and roughly 20% fuel savings, with sea trials planned for 2026 on a bulk carrier.

Two things to know before crediting this
  • The counterparty is a K Line subsidiary. Kawasaki Kisen Kaisha established OCEANICWING S.A.S. in France on 18 January 2024 and completed the acquisition of the AirSeas business on 15 February 2024. ALUULA's own materials name the counterparty as "OCEANICWING SAS (Airseas)." Better credit — and single-shipowner demand concentration dressed as a market.
  • The regulatory driver slipped by more than two years. At the October 2025 extraordinary IMO session, 57 member states voted to adjourn adoption of the Net-Zero Framework for a year against 49 to continue. Talks resume in October 2026 and the earliest possible implementation is now 1 March 2028. Every wind-assisted-propulsion market forecast published before that vote assumed the driver arrived on schedule.

What survives the slip: EU ETS reaches 100% of covered emissions on 1 January 2026, with methane and N₂O added; FuelEU Maritime penalties for the first compliance year were issued by 30 June 2026; and the IMO carbon intensity indicator tightens to roughly 11% below the 2019 baseline in 2026, heading to 21.5% by 2030. Wind propulsion earns a reward factor in the FuelEU GHG-intensity calculation. The installed base is real and growing: per the International Windship Association, over 100 ships and more than 230 wind propulsion systems as of 1 June 2026, growing at roughly double year-on-year rates.

The caveat that matters for ALUULA specifically: only towing kites are a fabric market. Flettner rotors, suction sails and rigid wingsails are steel and glass-reinforced-plastic structures and are not addressable. IWSA does not publish the technology-level split of the 230 installed systems, so the kite share of that installed base is not known.

What the incumbents are saying about defence demand is worth reading directly, because it is the demand signal ALUULA is not yet capturing. Avient's chief executive stated in February 2026 that defence demand for its products "grew double digits in 2024, followed by high single digits growth in 2025," and that incremental capital would be deployed over two years to expand Dyneema capacity — primarily by optimising existing lines rather than building new facilities. On the Q2 2026 call, reported the same day as this report's data cut-off, defence was guided to mid-to-high single-digit growth for the full year against a 19% comparable.

So: the defence UHMWPE market is growing at high single digits, the incumbent is adding capacity by debottlenecking rather than building, and ALUULA has no ballistic product, no qualification and no Berry compliance. The realistic competitive read is that ALUULA does not compete with Dyneema in armour and is unlikely to in this cycle. Where it does compete — ultralight laminates for packs, tents, sails and inflatable structures — the incumbent is DCF at 1.37 metres and Challenge ULTRA, and there the fused mono-polymer construction and the coming 1.5-metre width are genuine advantages.

For reference on the incumbent's pricing: DCF retails at roughly US$35 per square metre for the lightest 17 gsm grade and US$45–48 for heavier hybrids, against conventional coated nylons at 80–100 gsm. Finished gear in DCF typically costs 50–75% more than conventional equivalents for a 25–50% weight saving. That price umbrella is what makes a challenger economically viable at all.

§ 11Growth model

ALUULA publishes no revenue guidance, no profitability target and no capacity figure in revenue terms. The only forward quantitative statements the company makes are the 40–45% gross margin band, the order book, and the "~10x capacity" multiple. Everything below is therefore constructed, and labelled as such.

The construction logic is deliberately mechanical. Fiscal 2026 is capacity-bound: Victoria is at 100% utilisation, Q2 annualises to about C$12.2 million, and Vancouver is not operational until the last weeks of the fiscal year at the earliest. So FY2026 lands between C$11.0 and C$12.2 million regardless of demand. Fiscal 2027 is the first year in which the expansion can contribute, and the spread between scenarios is almost entirely a question of how fast a new plant fills.

§ 12Valuation: what is already in the price

At C$3.70 and 30,891,911 shares, ALUULA's market capitalisation is C$114.3 million. Net of C$13.66 million of cash and C$0.50 million of debt, enterprise value is approximately C$101 million. Against trailing revenue of C$10.43 million that is 9.7× EV/sales.

Three honest observations about that multiple.

It is defensible on growth, and only on growth. No company in the comparable set is growing at 80–94%. 5N Plus at 6.43× is the closest, and it is itself a re-rated stock, up 312% over a year, with three-year forecast growth in the low teens. If you believe FY2027 revenue of C$19 million, today's C$101 million enterprise value is 5.3× forward sales. If you believe C$24 million, it is 4.2×. Neither is absurd for a 43%-gross-margin business doubling in size. The multiple is not the problem; the confidence interval around the denominator is.

There is no published benchmark for what this cohort should trade at. No bank, index provider or data vendor publishes an EV/sales series for sub-US$200 million materials companies growing above 50% with margins above 40%. The cohort is too small and too illiquid to be indexed. Anyone quoting a multiple for it is asserting. The nearest credible anchors are Damodaran's January 2026 industry averages: specialty chemicals 2.65×, aerospace and defence 3.57×, whole US market 3.97×.

The scarcity premium is real and it is fragile. There is no other listed pure-play fused UHMWPE laminate company. That uniqueness supports a premium in a market looking for advanced-materials and defence-adjacent exposure. It also means there is no anchor when sentiment turns, and on 12,000 shares a day there is no mechanism for a valuation floor to assert itself.

What the price implies

Reverse-engineering: at a 4.0× exit multiple — a generous but not heroic level given the comps above — today's C$101 million enterprise value implies roughly C$25 million of revenue. That is between the base and bull FY2028 scenarios. So the market is currently paying today for revenue the company might reach in two to three years, assuming the Vancouver plant works and fills. That is a normal amount of forward-pull for a high-growth microcap. It also means there is no valuation cushion for execution slippage: a two-quarter delay in Vancouver does not reduce the terminal case, but it removes the compounding that justifies the entry multiple.

§ 16Glossary

UHMWPE
Ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene. A polymer whose extremely long molecular chains give it a strength-to-weight ratio roughly eight times that of steel. Sold as fibre (Dyneema, Spectra), as tape (Endumax), and as laminate. It does not absorb water, resists UV and chemicals, and melts at a relatively low temperature — which is what makes welding possible and what limits high-temperature applications.
Fusion vs adhesive lamination
Conventional UHMWPE laminates bond a fibre grid between polyester films with glue. ALUULA fuses a stack in which every layer is polyethylene, joined without adhesive. The practical differences are delamination resistance, weldability instead of stitching, and recyclability.
GSM / areal density
Grams per square metre. The standard weight measure for technical fabrics. ALUULA's range runs from 25–35 gsm for ultralight tents and sails to 155–220 gsm for rafts and shelters. Lower is lighter; the engineering question is always what strength survives at the lower number.
DCF — Dyneema Composite Fabric
The incumbent ultralight UHMWPE laminate, originally sold as Cuben Fiber, acquired by DSM Dyneema and now owned by Avient. Manufactured in the USA at 54 inches (1.372 m) wide. The reference product against which ALUULA's outdoor line is judged.
Roll width
The width at which a laminate is manufactured. Determines what patterns a customer can cut without seaming or waste. 1.5 metres is the technical-textile standard. ALUULA produces 0.925 m today and is building for 1.5 m — see §05.
Berry Amendment
10 U.S.C. §4862. Requires the US Department of Defense to buy US-grown or US-produced food, clothing, fibers, yarns and fabrics. A statutory bar, not a preference, on non-US textile inputs to DoD programmes.
SAFE
Security Action for Europe. The €150 billion loan instrument within the €800 billion ReArm Europe / Readiness 2030 package. Requires that non-European inputs not exceed 35% of a procured system's value.
Controlled Goods Program (CGP)
Canada's domestic industrial security regime governing the examination, possession and transfer of controlled goods, including defence articles. CGP registration is what supports the Canadian exemption under ITAR §126.5 that allows many defence articles to move between Canada and the US without a licence.
V50
The velocity at which a projectile penetrates an armour material 50% of the time. The fundamental ballistic performance metric. ALUULA has published none.
Income before interest, tax, amortization
ALUULA's own MD&A subtotal. It is not EBITDA. It is struck after share-based compensation and before other income. Q1 FY2026: (C$260,806). Q2 FY2026: (C$424,678). Any figure labelled EBITDA for this company came from a data vendor's calculation, not from the company.
LIFE offering
Listed Issuer Financing Exemption. A Canadian prospectus exemption allowing a listed issuer to raise a limited amount from the public with freely tradeable shares and no hold period. ALUULA used it for the February 2026 C$14.1M placement.
Share consolidation
A reverse split. ALUULA consolidated 20-to-1 effective 12 March 2025. Every share count, warrant exercise price and per-share figure before that date is on the old basis — the single most common source of error in third-party data on this name.
Wind-assisted ship propulsion (WASP)
Retrofit or newbuild systems that use wind to reduce a vessel's fuel consumption. Four technologies: towing kites, Flettner rotors, suction sails, rigid wingsails. Only towing kites are a fabric market.
CII / FuelEU / EU ETS
The three shipping decarbonisation instruments actually in force. CII is the IMO's carbon intensity indicator, tightening to ~11% below the 2019 baseline in 2026. FuelEU Maritime sets GHG intensity limits with a reward factor for wind propulsion; first penalties were issued by 30 June 2026. EU ETS reaches 100% of covered emissions on 1 January 2026.
EV / sales
Enterprise value divided by revenue. Used instead of price/earnings when a company is unprofitable. Enterprise value is market capitalisation plus debt minus cash — what an acquirer would pay for the whole business.
