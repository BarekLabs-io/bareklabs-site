## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The global space economy generated **$613 billion in 2024**, growing 7.8% year-on-year, with commercial activity representing 78% of the total and government budgets the remaining 22% ($132 billion, of which the U.S. accounts for $77 billion) [^88]. Novaspace pegs 2025 at **$626.4 billion** [^81]. Every major forecaster expects the market to roughly double within a decade: Space Foundation sees $1 trillion crossed as soon as 2032, Novaspace models $1.01 trillion by 2034, and the McKinsey/World Economic Forum partnership projects **$1.8 trillion by 2035** — a ~9% compound annual growth rate from the $630 billion estimated for 2023, well above global GDP growth [^88][^81][^25]. For calibration, SpaceX's own S-1 pitched a $28.5 trillion "total addressable market" — a figure NYU's Aswath Damodaran said "borders on fantasy," with nearly $26 trillion of it AI TAM that has nothing to do with space [^17][^21]. Serious investors should anchor on the $0.6–1.8 trillion range, not the $28.5 trillion marketing number.

**Four bullets that carry the thesis:**

- **The cost of orbit fell ~95% in a generation and is about to fall again.** Space Shuttle-era launch cost ~$54,500/kg to LEO (2026 dollars); a reused Falcon 9 delivers for ~$2,400–2,700/kg; Starship's design target is below $100/kg [^53][^54]. Every business model in this note is a derivative of that cost curve. On 24 July 2026, Starship Flight 13 deployed 20 next-generation Starlink V3 satellites and returned the upper stage intact for the first time — the vehicle that makes the next cost leg down real is now flying production hardware [^3].
- **Starlink proved a space business can be a great business, full stop.** FY2025: $11.4 billion revenue, $4.42 billion operating income, ~39% operating margin, 8.9 million subscribers — the first space-native franchise with software-like economics [^37]. It is the proof of concept the entire sector trades on. It is also, quietly, seeing ARPU fall from $99/month (2023) to $66/month (Q1 2026) as growth shifts to lower-income markets — volume is now doing all the work [^5][^37].
- **The SpaceX IPO reorganized the sector's capital structure — painfully.** The "halo trade" ran RKLB to $150.23 (27 May 2026) and ASTS to $133.09 (28 May 2026); the "great siphon" then cut both roughly in half in seven weeks as capital rotated into the real thing [^26] [Source: yahoo_finance — Yahoo Finance historical prices, as of 3 Aug 2026]. SPCX itself peaked at $225.64 intraday on 16 June and now trades at $114.53, 15% below its IPO price, with a **~$100 billion lockup expiry landing on 6 August 2026, days after this note** [^44][^45].
- **Defense has quietly become the sector's demand floor.** The U.S. Golden Dome missile-defense architecture carries a CBO estimate of **$1.2 trillion over 20 years**, ~70% of acquisition cost concentrated in a space-based interceptor constellation of ~7,800 satellites [^60][^65]. Rocket Lab is already on the Space Based Interceptor program with Raytheon; AST SpaceMobile is winning U.S. government awards through primes [^43][^40]. Whether or not Golden Dome survives budget scrutiny, the direction of U.S. space spending — proliferation, responsiveness, LEO militarization — is now the sector's most reliable tailwind.

**Why now?** Three inflections are converging in H2 2026. Technological: Starship V3 is flying with production Starlink V3 payloads, and Rocket Lab's Neutron — the first credible Western challenger to Falcon 9 in medium lift — is "on track for its debut launch later this year" [^43][^3]. Economic: the sector has its first profitable scaled franchise (Starlink), its first public pure-play leader (SPCX), and its first real comparable set — meaning capital can finally be allocated rather than merely bet. Political: Golden Dome, China's Guowang/Qianfan build-out (350+ satellites launched combined), and Europe's long-delayed IRIS² sovereignty constellation have made orbit a contested strategic domain in a way it has not been since the 1960s [^136][^62][^60].

## 2. HISTORY & CONTEXT — HOW A "NOT SO NEW" INDUSTRY GOT ITS SECOND WIND

### 2.1 From Apollo to stagnation: the half-century cost plateau

The space industry is old. Apollo put twelve men on the Moon between 1969 and 1972; the Space Shuttle flew 135 missions between 1981 and 2011. What never happened, across five decades, was a fall in the price of reaching orbit. The Shuttle was sold to Congress in the 1970s on the promise of ~$600/kg to LEO; it actually delivered at roughly **$54,500/kg** in today's money — nearly 100 times the projection [^53]. The expendable generation that followed was little better: Delta IV Heavy and Atlas V sit in a ~$13,000–14,000/kg band, and even today's Ariane 6 is near ~$7,500/kg [^53]. Launch was a government-procurement oligopoly — cost-plus contracts, no reuse, no cadence — and it priced orbit as a luxury good. The consequences cascaded: satellites had to be exquisite, billion-dollar, 15-year assets because replacement was unaffordable; constellations were uneconomic; and the "space economy" remained a rounding error of government budgets and GEO TV relays.

The stagnation was institutional, not physical. Nothing about rocket equation physics required $50,000/kg; the price reflected a market structure in which the customer (government) did not care about price and the supplier (a cost-plus prime) had no incentive to lower it. What SpaceX proved after its 2002 founding — and what took the industry two decades to internalize — is that launch is a manufacturing problem, and manufacturing problems yield to iteration, vertical integration, and volume.

### 2.2 The revolutionary factor: reusability as an economic engine

SpaceX landed its first orbital-class booster in December 2015 and has since normalized what was once science fiction: **85%+ of its missions now fly on previously flown boosters**, and it completed its ~650th Falcon-family launch around IPO day, 12 June 2026 [^37][^24]. The economics are compounding. A Falcon 9 comes in near **$1,500–2,700/kg** to LEO depending on reuse configuration versus ~$54,500 for the Shuttle — a decline of ~95% or more [^53][^54]. SpaceX completed **165 launches in 2025** — nearly eight times the next-busiest Western provider, Rocket Lab's record 21 [^28]. In the first half of 2025 the world was averaging one orbital launch every 28 hours, with SpaceX flying 81 of the 149 missions [^88].

Reusability did three things beyond price. It created **cadence** (reuse means your fleet grows with your launch rate, not your factory rate), it created **schedule reliability** (the scarcest commodity for constellation operators), and it created a **self-funding deployment machine**: SpaceX launches its own Starlinks at marginal cost, which no competitor can match. That vertical loop — rockets build the constellation, the constellation's cash flow builds the next rocket — is the structural core of the entire New Space economy.

### 2.3 Starship and the next cost leg

Starship is the bet that reusability economics extend to a **fully and rapidly reusable** super-heavy vehicle: 100–150+ tonnes to LEO at a targeted marginal cost below **$100/kg** [^53][^54]. As of 4 August 2026 the program is mid-maturation, not finished. Flight 12 (22 May 2026) was the first V3 flight — new Raptor 3 engines, new Pad 2 — and suffered engine-out anomalies and a failed booster splashdown [^8]. Flight 13 (24 July 2026, after a 16 July abort for four engines failing to ignite) was materially better: all 33 Raptor 3s lit, 20 production Starlink V3 satellites deployed and briefly networked with the constellation, an in-space Raptor relight, and — for the first time — an intact ship after splashdown, giving engineers their first clean look at a flown heatshield [^3][^13][^1]. The booster was again lost to engine relight failures at splashdown [^3]. Flight 14 (Booster 21/Ship 41) is targeted around September 2026 with the first attempt at a stable orbit and a tower "chopstick" catch of the upper stage [^90].

The market implication is binary and underappreciated: **Starship is not currently needed for SpaceX's existing economics — it is the option on the next ones.** Starlink V3 satellites (10x capacity), the second-generation direct-to-cell system (150 Mbps/user target), orbital AI compute, and the entire $100/kg thesis all require Starship to work at cadence [^124][^59]. Flight 13 suggests the physics is cooperating; the two lost boosters say the economics are not yet bankable. SpaceX spent ~$3 billion on Starship R&D in 2025 alone [^37].

### 2.4 The Starlink inflection: the sector's proof of concept

Before Starlink, "space" for public investors meant defense primes and fading GEO broadcasters. Starlink changed the ontology: **a space-native service business with recurring revenue, consumer scale, and 39% operating margins.** FY2025: $11.4 billion revenue (+49.8% YoY), $4.42 billion operating income (+120%), $7.2 billion segment adjusted EBITDA (+86%) [^37]. Subscribers went 2.3M (2023) → 4.4M (2024) → 8.9M (2025) → **10.3 million at 31 March 2026** across 155 countries, with Quilty Space projecting 16.8 million by year-end 2026 [^37][^7]. Starlink now generates the majority of SpaceX's revenue (61% in 2025, 69% in Q1 2026) and all of its profit [^7].

The bear wrinkle, visible for the first time in S-1 data, is **ARPU erosion**: $99/month (2023) → $91 (2024) → $81 (2025) → $86 (Q1 2025) → **$66 in Q1 2026** — down 23% year-on-year as subscriber growth shifts to price-sensitive markets in Africa, Southeast Asia, and Latin America [^37][^5]. Operating income in Q1 2026 rose only $160 million on a doubled subscriber base [^5]. SpaceX responded in May 2026 by raising consumer plan prices $5–10/month — a pivot from land-grab to harvest [^5]. Starlink remains a superb business; the point is that even the sector's best asset now has a visible law-of-large-numbers problem — and the market still prices the whole company at more than 70x trailing revenue anyway [^45].

### 2.5 Geopolitics: orbit is a contested domain again

The demand side of space has been rewritten by strategic competition. In the U.S., the signature program is **Golden Dome**: a homeland missile-defense architecture ordered by executive order on 27 January 2025, publicly scoped at $175 billion and "fully operational in three years" by President Trump in May 2025 — and estimated by the CBO on 12 May 2026 to actually cost **$1.2 trillion over 20 years**, with ~70% of acquisition spending absorbed by a **space-based interceptor (SBI) constellation of ~7,800 satellites** (~$720 billion including ~1,600 replenishment satellites per year at a five-year service life) [^60]. Program chief Gen. Michael Guetlein has publicly pushed back on the CBO figure — "They're not estimating what we're building" — while raising his own estimate to $185 billion, leaving Golden Dome simultaneously the sector's largest potential procurement ever and a genuine affordability binary [^65]. The demand signal is broader than interceptors: proliferated LEO missile-warning, responsive launch (Rocket Lab's VICTUS HAZE flew **16 hours 42 minutes after notice** in June 2026), and hybrid commercial-military networks [^51].

China is the pacing threat and the under-covered angle for Western investors. LandSpace's **Zhuque-3** — a methalox reusable rocket explicitly modeled on Falcon 9 economics — flew its maiden mission on 3 December 2025 (orbit achieved; booster recovery failed), with a second flight and landing attempt planned for H2 2026 and a first reflight targeted for Q4 [^67]. On-orbit, China's two state-backed constellations, Guowang and Qianfan, had launched **350+ satellites combined by mid-2026** [^136]. Europe, meanwhile, illustrates how hard sovereignty is to buy: the **IRIS²** secure-connectivity constellation (~290 satellites, services from 2030) saw the SpaceRISE consortium (Eutelsat/SES/Hispasat) sign its concession agreement only on 17 July 2026, years behind the original schedule — a reminder that announced government demand is not the same as executed contracts [^62].

The investment translation: U.S. and allied defense demand is the one revenue pool in this sector that is growing, budget-backed, and largely price-insensitive. Every company in this note is repositioning toward it. SpaceX's S-1 discloses that **roughly one-fifth of 2025 revenue came from U.S. federal agencies** [^59]; Rocket Lab's backlog and M&A are now defense-led; AST SpaceMobile won three new U.S. government awards in Q1 2026 alone [^43][^40].

### 3.1 SpaceX / SPCX — the sector's gravity well, now with a ticker

Musk controls ~85% of the vote through super-voting stock; public float holders own an economic claim, not a governance one [^17].

**Financials (first-ever disclosure, S-1 filed 20 May 2026).** FY2025: revenue **$18.7 billion (+33%)**, net loss **-$4.9 billion** (vs +$791 million profit in 2024), adjusted EBITDA $6.6 billion, operating loss -$2.6 billion, capex $20.7 billion, long-term debt $29.1 billion [^37]. ### 3.2 Rocket Lab / RKLB — the anti-SpaceX, at a SpaceX price

**Business model.** Two integrated segments. **Launch Services:** Electron (59 missions flown; 21 in 2025, a record, including 3 HASTE hypersonic variants; 16 in 2024), the second-most-frequently launched U.S. orbital rocket, plus the suborbital HASTE hypersonic test vehicle; and **Neutron**, the 13,000kg-to-LEO reusable medium-lift rocket now in final qualification, "on track for debut launch later this year" (2026), and deliberately priced below Falcon 9's sticker [^43]. **Space Systems:** satellite components (reaction wheels, solar power, separation systems, software) and full Photon spacecraft — the segment that makes Rocket Lab an end-to-end "space prime in miniature," supplying components to missions including its competitors'. The strategic logic: launch is lumpy and low-margin; components ride every program in the industry, including Golden Dome.

**Financial trajectory.** Revenue: $62.2M (2021) → $211.0M (2022) → $244.6M (2023) → $436.2M (2024) → **$601.8M (2025, +38%)** [Source: sp_data — S&P Capital IQ standardized financials, FY2021–FY2025]. Q1 2026 (filed 7 May 2026): revenue **$200.3M, +63.5% YoY**; GAAP gross margin 38.2% (non-GAAP 43.0%); adjusted EBITDA -$11.75M; net loss -$45.0M; operating cash flow -$50.3M [Source: sp_data — S&P Capital IQ quarterly financials, Q1 2026] [^43]. Consensus has FY2026 at ~$924M, FY2027 at ~$1.29B, FY2028 at ~$1.70B, with adjusted EBITDA turning positive in 2027 (+$120M) and reaching ~$293M in 2028 [Source: sp_data — S&P Capital IQ consensus estimates, effective Feb–Mar 2026].

**Backlog, balance sheet, and the defense pivot.** Backlog hit a record **$2.2 billion, up 20% in a single quarter**, including 31 new Electron/HASTE missions and **5 dedicated Neutron launches signed before first flight**; the company sold more launches in Q1 2026 than in all of 2025, for a manifest exceeding 70 contracted missions [^43]. The U.S. government is now the growth engine: selection alongside Raytheon to demonstrate capabilities for the U.S. Space Force's Space Based Interceptor program, a $30 million Anduril award for HASTE hypersonic test flights, and responsive-launch credibility (VICTUS HAZE: contract to orbit in 16h42m, a world record) [^43][^51]. **Neutron is the whole ballgame.** Rocket Lab keeps its "first launch in 2026" language, and five customers have already signed dedicated Neutron missions [^43]. Success makes RKLB the only Western alternative to Falcon 9 in medium lift with real cadence economics — a strategic asset the U.S. government actively wants to exist. Slippage into 2027, or a first-flight failure, would hit a stock trading at ~42x 2026E revenue. Electron's economics, by contrast, are structurally capped: ~$23,000/kg is a premium niche product — responsive, dedicated small launch — not a mass-market one.

### 3.3 AST SpaceMobile / ASTS — the highest-beta call option in public markets

**Business model.** AST SpaceMobile is building the first and only space-based cellular broadband network designed to connect **unmodified smartphones** — no dish, no special chip — directly to satellites, in partnership with mobile network operators (MNOs) who wholesale capacity to their own subscribers. The partner roster is the asset: ~60 MNOs covering ~3 billion subscribers, anchored by AT&T, Verizon and Vodafone (long-term revenue-shares), with Google and American Tower as investors, plus Rakuten, stc and recent additions Telus and Axian [^40]. Spectrum position: ~3,900 patent claims; an FCC Supplemental Coverage from Space (SCS) grant authorizing up to **248 satellites** for U.S. service [^40]. Technology milestones are real: **98.9 Mbps peak download** demonstrated to a standard smartphone from a Block 1 BlueBird in May 2026 — broadband-class speeds from orbit to a phone in your pocket [^40].

**The revenue inflection is behind the "pre-revenue" label — but only just.** FY2025 revenue: **$70.9M** (vs $4.4M FY2024) — gateway equipment sales, government milestones, and early partner prepayments recognized; FY2025 net loss -$341.9M; operating cash flow -$71.5M; investing outflow -$1.54B; financing inflow +$3.83B [Source: sp_data — S&P Capital IQ standardized financials, FY2025]. Q1 2026 (filed 11 May 2026): revenue $14.7M; operating expenses $164.1M (including $73M of D&A and stock comp); net loss -$191.0M; cash ~$3.5B including restricted after another +$1.1B financing quarter; **FY2026 guidance $150–200M, supported by the existing contracted pipeline**; management describes a 2027 revenue opportunity approaching $1 billion, long-term contracted or highly recurring in nature [Source: sp_data — S&P Capital IQ quarterly financials, Q1 2026] [^40]. Consensus: 2026E revenue $168.8M, EBITDA -$264M; 2027E revenue $690.5M, EBITDA +$287M; 2028E revenue $1.81B, EBITDA ~$1.5B — the street is modeling one of the steepest EBITDA ramps ever assigned to a hardware company [Source: sp_data — S&P Capital IQ consensus estimates, effective Feb–Mar 2026].

**The constellation is a schedule story with a dependency problem.** On orbit today: BlueWalker 3 (2022) + 5 Block 1 BlueBirds (September 2024) + BB6 (24 December 2025, on ISRO's LVM3) + BB8–10 (17 June 2026, Falcon 9) = **10 satellites**. **BB7 was lost on 19 April 2026** when Blue Origin's New Glenn upper stage malfunctioned — $155–160M of carrying value, insured, with a replacement launch secured [^40]. BB11–13 are scheduled to fly **5 August 2026 — tomorrow — aboard a Falcon 9**, with ~45 satellites targeted by year-end 2026 and beta commercial service "later this year" [^35][^40]. The structural irony deserves emphasis: **ASTS's fastest path to orbit is a rocket owned by its most dangerous competitor.** SpaceX's T-Satellite direct-to-cell service (commercial since July 2025, $10/month with T-Mobile, ~650 D2C-capable satellites, a 150 Mbps/user second generation in development) competes head-on for the same unmodified-phone market — and SpaceX's "Starlink Mobile" trademark filings signal it may one day sell direct [^79][^124]. ASTS's counter-position: deeper MNO revenue-shares, superior per-satellite throughput (BlueBird arrays are far larger), and wholesale-neutrality — it sells capacity to carriers, while T-Satellite makes SpaceX itself the carrier.

> **KPI — Cash runway.** *Definition:* how long current liquidity funds operations at the present burn rate. *Formula:* (cash + short-term investments) ÷ |annual operating + investing cash outflow|. *Why it matters here:* ASTS's ~$3.0B of cash and ST investments against a 2025 operating+investing burn of ~$1.6B gives under two years of full-tilt constellation deployment — the company must hit the 2027 revenue ramp or return to capital markets, and its ~$4.9B of financing inflows over the past 15 months show how actively it uses that window [Source: sp_data — S&P Capital IQ standardized financials, FY2025 and Q1 2026].

**Valuation.** At $63.52 (3 August): market cap $24.65B, EV $19.5B, **230x TTM revenue**, ~115x 2026E revenue, ~28x 2027E revenue; beta 2.75; short interest ~20% of float — one of the most heavily shorted large-caps in the U.S. market; shares -52% from the 28 May 2026 ATH of $133.09, +72% above the 52-week low of $36.91 (9 September 2025); YTD -23.9%; 11 analysts, mean target $80.48 (consensus hold); Q2 earnings 10 August [Source: yahoo_finance — Yahoo Finance quote, historical price and analyst data, as of 3 Aug 2026]. ASTS is the cleanest expression of the sector's core trade: enormous verified technology, binary schedule risk, a debt-heavy balance sheet (cash $3.03B vs debt $2.99B), and a valuation that requires the 2027–2028 ramp to arrive on time.

## 5. COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE — THE REST OF THE CHESSBOARD

### 5.1 Launch: everyone is racing to be second

The launch market is a monopoly with a queue of aspirants. SpaceX's 165 launches in 2025 were nearly eight times the next-busiest Western provider — Rocket Lab's record 21 [^28]. **Blue Origin's New Glenn** has flown only three times, and its NG-3 upper-stage failure in April 2026 destroyed ASTS's BB7 satellite — a reminder that reliability is the product, and Blue Origin isn't shipping it yet [^135]. **Relativity's Terran R** and **Firefly/Northrop's Eclipse** remain in development, with first flights targeted from late 2026 onward [^53]. Europe's **Ariane 6** targets roughly $7,500/kg to LEO at ~12 flights a year — a 3–5x price disadvantage that relegates it to assured-access missions [^53]. The realistic read: **Neutron is the only 2026–2027 entrant with signed customers, a test-fired engine (Archimedes), and a company whose survival depends on cadence rather than cost-plus** [^43]. China's Zhuque-3 is the true long-run competitor — it is copying the Falcon 9 playbook with state patience and a domestic constellation pipeline (Guowang, Qianfan) to feed it [^67][^136].

### 5.2 Direct-to-cell: the fight for the unmodified phone

The D2C market — satellite connectivity straight to standard handsets — is where the sector's next margin pool forms. Four architectures are competing:

| Player | Architecture | Status (Aug 2026) | Carrier strategy | Key vulnerability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **SpaceX T-Satellite** | ~650 D2C satellites; 2nd-gen targeting 150 Mbps/user [^124] | Commercial since July 2025; $10/month with T-Mobile [^79] | Wholesale to carriers (T-Mobile, KDDI, Optus…); "Starlink Mobile" trademark filed | ~4 Mbps per user today; capacity limited in dense areas |
| **AST SpaceMobile** | 10 satellites on orbit (BB7 lost on New Glenn, April 2026); BB11–BB13 launch 5 Aug on Falcon 9, further missions on New Glenn and ISRO's LVM3 [^35][^134] | FCC commercial authorization granted April 2026 [^129]; 98 Mbps demoed to an unmodified phone [^124]; ~3 billion subscribers under MNO partner umbrellas (AT&T, Verizon, Vodafone, Rakuten, stc) | Wholesale to MNOs (revenue share) | Only ~10 satellites on orbit vs ~45–60 needed for continuous CONUS coverage [^120] |
| **Globalstar (+Apple; Amazon)** | MSS spectrum in 100+ countries | iPhone satellite features live; Amazon acquisition announced April 2026 [^112] | Wholesale to device/platform owners | Feature-limited today; ownership in flux |
| **Amazon Leo** (ex-Kuiper) | 153 satellites (1 Aug 2026) vs 578 required under its FCC license [^50] | Enterprise/government beta; extension of the 30 July 2026 FCC deadline requested [^50] | Vertical integration (retail, AWS, logistics) | Years behind Starlink; launch capacity contracted through 2027; buys rides on rivals' rockets [^112] |

The strategic tell is spectrum: SpaceX's "Starlink Mobile" trademark filings signal it may eventually sell service directly rather than only through carriers [^79], while Amazon's announced Globalstar acquisition buys it MSS spectrum in more than 100 countries [^112]. ASTS's counter is contractual — carriers don't want to be disintermediated by SpaceX, and ASTS is the only scaled alternative that lets them keep the customer. That dynamic, more than the technology race, is why ASTS trades at 230x sales.

### 5.3 The incumbents and the scoreboard

The old space economy still dwarfs the new in revenue: estimated 2025 space-related revenues of roughly Airbus Defense & Space ~$14B, Lockheed Martin ~$12B, Northrop Grumman ~$9.2B, Boeing ~$5.8B — against SpaceX's ~$15B of space-native revenue, which passed every one of them [^81]. The pure-play public bench beyond our three names trades at a wide multiple dispersion (Section 8.2), from Viasat at 3.5x EV/S to Globalstar at ~39x [Source: yahoo_finance — Yahoo Finance quote data, as of 3 Aug 2026]. Two structural notes. First, **GEO is dying**: Viasat's multiple is the market pricing secular decline of broadcast/broadband GEO against LEO substitution. Second, **Earth observation remains a buyer's market** — Planet (PL) at 22x EV/S with improving margins shows data businesses can work, but none has yet found Starlink-scale consumer demand.

### 6.2 Risk factors, ranked by how mispriced we think they are

**1. The lockup/mechanical risk (most immediate, most quantifiable).** A ~$100 billion unlock into a stock with a 5.36 beta is not a sentiment risk — it is a supply/demand mechanical fact. Morgan Stanley's own language — the stock could be "at the mercy" of technical factors — is as close to a sell warning as a lead underwriter issues, and options markets were pricing roughly 149% annualized volatility for the expiry week [^44][^45]. If SPCX gaps down on the unlock, RKLB and ASTS will follow (July correlation: they fell together).

**2. Kessler syndrome and orbital carrying capacity.** There are **29,219 tracked objects larger than 10 cm in orbit — only about 18,500 of them active satellites** — and 306 orbital launches year-to-date 2026 alone [^151][^81]. SpaceX operates ~two-thirds of all active satellites and **self-insures its constellation and vehicles** [^37] — a choice that is accretive in quiet years and existential in a cascade scenario. The FCC's 5-year deorbit rule raises compliance costs sector-wide. A single major fragmentation event in a crowded shell would reprice the entire LEO thesis overnight, with no hedging instrument available. This is the sector's true systemic risk and it is priced at zero.

**3. ITAR/export controls and geopolitical bifurcation.** U.S. space technology is export-controlled (ITAR/EAR); the commercial consequence is a bifurcated world market in which U.S. players cannot sell into China and Chinese constellations (Guowang, Qianfan) build a parallel ecosystem [^67]. For RKLB, ITAR constrains the addressable launch market; for ASTS, MNO partnerships in sensitive jurisdictions carry compliance overhead; for the sector, "space sovereignty" spending (IRIS²'s July 2026 concession signing, Golden Dome) is the offsetting tailwind [^136][^62].

**4. Capital-intensity/dilution risk (the slow killer).** Every company in this note except mature-Starlink burns cash: RKLB diluted +5.9% in one quarter; ASTS has raised ~$4.9B in 15 months and carries $2.99B of debt against $3.03B of cash; SpaceX itself spent $20.7B of capex in FY2025 against $6.6B of adjusted EBITDA [^43][^40][^37]. In a risk-off tape, the dilution engine reverses violently.

**5. The demand-substitution risk nobody models: Amazon.** Amazon Leo has 153 satellites up against a 578-satellite FCC requirement — with an extension requested on its 30 July 2026 deployment deadline — plus the announced Globalstar acquisition for MSS spectrum, and a parent with unlimited capital and its own consumer/enterprise distribution [^50][^112]. Leo does not need to beat Starlink to damage the sector — it only needs to cap pricing. Starlink's ARPU is already down by roughly a third from 2023 [^5].

**6. Key-person and governance concentration.** Musk holds ~85% voting power at SPCX; xAI merger terms were set by boards he influences; the Anthropic contract (xAI's largest) is 90-day cancellable [^16]. Single-point governance failure is a real scenario at a $1.4T asset.

## 7. INVESTMENT FRAMEWORK — HOW TO OWN THIS SECTOR WITHOUT OWNING ITS VOLATILITY

### 7.1 The three layers: infrastructure, services, applications

The sector divides into three risk/return layers, and portfolio construction should respect the hierarchy. **Infrastructure** (launch, manufacturing, components: SpaceX launch segment, RKLB launch + space systems, Firefly, Relativity) sells the picks and shovels — demand grows with every constellation regardless of which service wins, but margins are manufacturing margins and capital intensity is brutal. **Services** (connectivity: Starlink, ASTS, Amazon Leo, T-Satellite) own recurring revenue and pricing power once the constellation is amortized — this is where the 39% operating margins live — but requires surviving the capex valley of death first. **Applications** (Earth observation data, analytics: Planet, BlackSky) are asset-light but demand-constrained; none has yet proven consumer-scale pull. The historical lesson of every infrastructure wave (railroads, fiber, cloud) applies: builders get paid late, service owners get paid most, and application economics arrive last and thinnest.

The practical allocation conclusion: a balanced New Space book is long services-with-proven-unit-economics (SPCX's Starlink core), long infrastructure-with-defense-demand (RKLB's components/HASTE/Golden Dome exposure), and treats everything pre-inflection (ASTS's constellation, Neutron, Starship economics) as **option positions sized as options** — capital you can mark to zero without changing your life. The mistake the market made in H1 2026 was sizing option positions as core holdings; July was the correction of that error [Source: yahoo_finance — Yahoo Finance historical prices, July 2026].

### 7.2 Investor profiles and the beta problem

This sector is not one trade; it is at least four. **Growth-at-scale investors** (TAM compounders, 5–10yr horizons) belong in SPCX if — and only if — they underwrite the AI segment separately and are comfortable with Musk governance at 85% voting control [^16]. **Execution/milestone investors** (catalyst-driven, 6–24 month horizons) fit RKLB: Neutron first flight, backlog conversion, Golden Dome awards are discrete, dated, verifiable events. **Convexity seekers** fit ASTS: 20% short interest, 2.75 beta, a binary constellation ramp, and genuine technology make it the highest-convexity instrument in the sector — in both directions. **Value investors have no home here** — and should say so rather than pretending a 58x-sales stock is a value stock.

> **KPI — Beta.** *Definition:* a stock's sensitivity to the broad market. *Formula:* β = Cov(r_stock, r_market) / Var(r_market). *Why it matters here:* SPCX 5.36, ASTS 2.75, RKLB 2.63 [Source: yahoo_finance — Yahoo Finance risk metrics, as of 3 Aug 2026]. A 5.36 beta means a -10% Nasdaq month is, mechanically, a ~-50% SPCX month. Position sizing must start from beta, not conviction: at these betas, a 5% portfolio weight in SPCX contributes more market risk than a 25% weight in the index itself.

### 7.3 ESG: the uncomfortable ledger

Space scores badly on conventional ESG screens and investors should know exactly why rather than discover it in a compliance review. Environmental: launch emissions are trivial today but Starship at airline-like cadence is not; orbital debris (29,219 tracked objects over 10 cm, an estimated 1.2 million between 1 and 10 cm) is an accumulating externality the industry does not pay for; mega-constellation light pollution has triggered formal astronomy-community objections [^151]. Social: the connectivity dividend is real — Starlink's 155-country footprint and D2C emergency services (post-disaster deployments) are genuine public goods; counterbalanced by dual-use/military entanglement (Starlink in active conflict zones, Golden Dome's weaponization of LEO). Governance: this is the sector's weakest pillar — SpaceX's 85% voting concentration, related-party AI merger, and self-dealing optics would fail most institutional governance screens outright [^16]. The defensible ESG posture for institutions is engagement-on-governance plus acknowledgment that "space sustainability" (debris norms, deorbit compliance) will become a regulatory cost line within five years.

## 8. VALUATION & SCENARIOS

### 8.1 What the market is actually pricing

> **KPI — EV/Sales.** *Definition:* enterprise value (market cap + debt - cash) divided by revenue. *Formula:* EV/S = EV ÷ revenue (TTM or forward). *Why it matters here:* with none of the three profitable at group level, EV/S is the sector's common currency — and its dispersion (SPCX ~75x, RKLB ~58x, ASTS ~230x TTM) is the cleanest single map of relative crowding in the sector [Source: yahoo_finance — Yahoo Finance valuation data, as of 3 Aug 2026].

> **KPI — EV/EBITDA.** *Definition:* enterprise value divided by earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. *Formula:* EV/EBITDA = EV ÷ EBITDA. *Why it matters here:* the market's answer to "how many years of current cash earnings am I paying for?" SPCX trades at ~764x TTM EV/EBITDA; even on 2028 consensus, ASTS trades at ~13x and RKLB at ~135x — meaning buyers of RKLB today are underwriting flawless delivery of a 2028 EBITDA that itself assumes Neutron works [Source: yahoo_finance — Yahoo Finance valuation data, as of 3 Aug 2026] [Source: sp_data — S&P Capital IQ consensus estimates, effective Feb–Mar 2026].

> **KPI — Free cash flow.** *Definition:* cash generated after the capital expenditures needed to sustain the business. *Formula:* FCF = operating cash flow - capex. *Why it matters here:* the sector's dirty secret — SpaceX's FY2025 operating segments generated cash, but $20.7B of capex turned consolidated FCF deeply negative; RKLB's FCF is negative through at least 2026E; ASTS's until the 2027 ramp. This sector finances itself with equity and convertible debt, not internally generated cash, which is precisely why dilution (RKLB +5.9% shares in Q1 2026) is a structural feature, not a bug [^43]. SpaceX's $20.7B FY2025 capex figure is from its S-1 [^37].

### 8.4 SpaceX: what is $1.44 trillion actually made of?

Build it from parts. Starlink: 2026E revenue ~$14–15B growing ~30% with ~40% incremental margins; at a generous-but-defensible 15x forward sales (a multiple mature, high-growth connectivity franchises rarely exceed), that is ~$210–225B. Launch ex-Starship-option: dominant, strategic, ~$4B revenue, modest margins — worth $80–120B as a defense-adjacent utility. Starship option (the $100/kg future, V3 constellation capacity, orbital compute): genuinely valuable, genuinely unproven — call it $100–300B of option value. xAI: the Anthropic lease (up to $45 billion through May 2029) is xAI's largest contract and is 90-day cancellable; net the stake at $100–200B depending on your AI priors [^16]. Sum: **~$0.5–0.9 trillion of defensible value against a $1.44 trillion market cap.** Morningstar's $62-per-share fair value estimate lands squarely in that range; the $236.71 average analyst target requires Starship economics and orbital AI to both work [^22][^45]. Our scenario frame: **Bull ~$183 (+68%, ~$2.4T)** if Flight 14 catches the ship and Q2 shows AI capex discipline; **Base ~$110 (+1%, ~$1.44T)** — the market has roughly found fair speculative value; **Bear ~$59 (-46%)** — lockup supply + AI multiple compression, converging toward Morningstar's anchor.

### 8.5 The DCF honesty box

We built simplified two-stage DCFs for both public pure-plays (10-year explicit horizon, consensus-fed growth, 11–12% WACC, terminal growth 3%). Results: RKLB illustrative EV ≈ **$8.5B** vs actual EV $39.5B; ASTS ≈ **$8.8B** vs actual $19.5B. In other words, **no name in this sector is justifiable on 10 years of discounted cash flow** — the multiples embed value beyond year 10 (Starship-era cost curves, D2C at full constellation scale, Golden Dome at full procurement). That does not make the stocks wrong; it makes them **options on the 2035 economy, priced in 2026 dollars**, and it means the entire sector trades on narrative durability. When the narrative breaks — a failed Neutron debut, a Starship pad disaster, an AI capex revolt — there is no cash-flow floor. July 2026 was a dress rehearsal for what that looks like [Source: yahoo_finance — Yahoo Finance historical prices, July 2026].

## FINAL WORD

The New Space economy is real in a way it was not five years ago: $613 billion of revenue, a profitable constellation franchise, launch costs down 95%, and defense budgets now structurally allocated to orbit. But the *assets* through which public investors access it have been through a violent repricing: the SpaceX IPO created the sector's benchmark and simultaneously revealed that its proxies were, in Vanda Research's phrase, "transitional holdings" [^26]. Our stance, in one line each: **SPCX** — the franchise is the best in the sector and the price is the worst; wait for the lockup to clear before building a position. **RKLB** — the best-executing company relative to its plan, at a multiple that leaves no room for the plan to change; the Neutron debut is a coin-flip the stock cannot afford to lose. **ASTS** — the only name where this week's news (BB11–13, 5 August) can genuinely double the equity story or halve it; sized as an option, it is the most interesting risk/reward in the sector. The economy is inevitable. These entry points are not.
